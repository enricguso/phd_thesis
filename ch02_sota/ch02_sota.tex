\section{Deep learning for audio}
\label{sec:bg_dl}
%! TeX root: ../thesis_a4.tex


This section is intended as a non-exhaustive, deliberately synthetic recap
of the \acrshort{DNN} concepts needed to follow the rest of the thesis,
rather than a thorough survey. It is written primarily for the neophyte,
or for the audio researcher whose expertise lies in a neighbouring field\footnote{This section grew out of a series of internal seminars held within the
Multimedia Unit at Eurecat. Accordingly, the presentation prioritises intuition over rigour; the
interested reader is referred to standard texts for a complete treatment.}.

\subsection{The advent of DNNs (2014--2024)}
\label{sec:bg_dl_advent}

\textbf{The Perceptron ---} The basic unit of a neural network is the \emph{perceptron}~\citep{rosenblatt1958perceptron}, depicted in
Figure~\ref{fig:perceptron}. Given an input $x \in \mathcal{X}$ with
components $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the perceptron forms a weighted sum of its
inputs, adds a bias, and passes the result through a non-linear
\emph{activation} function $f$ to produce an output $o$:
\begin{equation}
    o = f_\theta(x) = f\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} w_k\, x_k + b\right),
    \qquad \theta = \{w_1, \dots, w_n,\, b\}.
    \label{eq:perceptron}
\end{equation}
The parameters $\theta$ --- the weights $w_k$ and the bias $b$ --- are
exactly the $\theta$ of Equation~\ref{eq:erm}: the perceptron is the
simplest instance of the parametric map $f_\theta : \mathcal{X}
\rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$ we wish to fit. 


\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{ch02_sota/figures/perceptron.pdf}
    \caption{The Perceptron, the basic unit of a neural network.}
    \label{fig:perceptron}
\end{figure}

Training begins from \emph{randomly initialised} weights and proceeds by
making the output $o$ progressively closer to a target $y$ by a \emph{loss} $\mathcal{L}(o, y)$ whose form depends on the task. For a
\emph{classification} task the target $y$ is a class label and a common
choice is the binary cross-entropy, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(o, y) = -\big[\, y \log o + (1-y)\log(1-o)\,\big] $, whereas for a \emph{regression} task --- the family this thesis focuses
on, where $y$ is itself a signal --- the loss measures a numerical
distance, such as the mean absolute error $ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MAE}}(o, y) = \lvert o - y \rvert $. 

The weights are then adjusted to reduce this loss by
\emph{back-propagation}, which computes the gradient of the loss with
respect to every parameter, followed by Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), which takes a small
step against that gradient: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(o, y) $, where $\eta$ is the learning rate. Because evaluating the expectation in Equation~\ref{eq:erm} over the whole dataset at once is impractical, in
practice SGD approximates it on small random subsets of the
data, the \emph{mini-batches}: each iteration of SGD is
computed on a fresh mini-batch, and the loop is repeated until the loss
converges. This cycle --- forward pass, loss, back-propagation,
parameter update --- is the engine behind every model in this thesis. Crucially, all of the above presupposes that for every input $x$ we have
access to its corresponding target $y$: it is the pairing $(x, y)$ that
makes the loss in Equation~\ref{eq:erm} computable. This is
known as \emph{supervised learning}. 

\textbf{The multi-layer perceptron}~\citep{rumelhart1986learning} stacks many perceptron units into
layers and composing them yields a \acrshort{DNN}, but the learning
principle is already visible in a single unit (the perceptron). 

\textbf{The \acrfull{CNN}}~\citep{lecun1998gradient} is a pivotal refinement: instead of learning an independent weight for every input--unit connection, we learn a set of
\emph{filters} (or \emph{kernels}) that are slid across the input, as
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cnn}. Each is applied at
every spatial location, so the same weights are reused throughout the
input --- a form of weight sharing that drastically reduces the number of
parameters and builds in translation equivariance. \acrshort{CNN}s were
first popularised for handwritten-digit recognition and, more than a
decade later, became the workhorse of computer vision.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{ch02_sota/figures/cnn_kernel.pdf}
    \caption{Convolutional filters in a \acrfull{CNN}. \textbf{(a)}~A
    $3\times3$ filter (kernel) slid across a $4\times4$ input image or feature map (blue) to produce a $2\times2$ output feature map (green); at each of
    the four positions the filter covers a local region of the input
    (highlighted) and produces a single output value. \textbf{(b)}~Illustrative examples of filters learned: the grayscale filters (top) resemble oriented Gabor functions acting as edge detectors, while the colour
    filters (bottom) capture colour-opponent and low-frequency patterns.}
    \label{fig:cnn}
\end{figure}


In audio, the two-dimensional input is not an image but a
spatio-temporal representation of the signal, most commonly the
Short-Time Fourier Transform. Early systems operated on the magnitude spectrogram
alone, discarding the phase, although recent models process
the complex spectrogram \citep{tong2024scnet, schroter2023deepfilternet3} --- real and imaginary parts jointly.

Sliding a filter over the input yields a smaller \emph{feature map}, one
per filter; stacking many filters per layer and many layers produces a
hierarchy of feature maps of increasing abstraction. While the
filters learned in the first layers of an image \acrshort{CNN} resemble
Gabor functions tuned to particularly oriented edge detectors
(Figure~\ref{fig:cnn}); in music, the analogous  filters
tend to specialise instead on timbre or rhythm. Deeper layers compose these primitives into progressively complex detectors
for more concrete concepts --- particular colours or shapes
in vision, or higher-level acoustic patterns in audio.

Formally, writing $\mathbf{H}^{(0)} = x$ for the input and $\ast$ for the
convolution, the $k$-th feature map of layer $l$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{H}^{(l)}_{k} = f\!\left(
        \sum_{c=1}^{C_{l-1}} \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{k,c} \ast \mathbf{H}^{(l-1)}_{c}
        + b^{(l)}_{k} \right),
    \label{eq:conv}
\end{equation}
where the learned filters $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{k,c}$ and biases $b^{(l)}_{k}$
now play the role of the parameters $\theta$ of Equation~\ref{eq:erm}, and
$f$ is the (non-linear) activation, typically a rectified linear unit.
After $L$ such layers, interleaved with pooling operations that
progressively reduce the spatial resolution, the final feature maps are
flattened into a single vector $
    z = \mathrm{vec}\!\left(\mathbf{H}^{(L)}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{d} $
known as the \emph{embedding}: a compact, learned representation of the
input that concentrates the information relevant to the task. The output
is then a simple --- often linear --- read-out of this embedding, $o = f_\theta(x) = g\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{o}\, z + \mathbf{b}_{o}\right)$
with $g$ a softmax over classes for a classification task. The embedding
is a central object in modern deep learning: because it distils the
task-relevant content of the input into a fixed-size vector, the same
embedding can often be reused for tasks other than the one it was trained
on --- an idea we return to when discussing pre-trained representations.

A canonical example is the VGG network~\citep{simonyan2014very}, sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:vgg16}: a deep stack
of small $3\times3$ convolutions and max-pooling layers that successively
halve the spatial resolution while increasing the number of feature maps,
followed by a few fully connected layers that map the final embedding to
the output classes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.8\textwidth, trim={.25cm 3cm .25cm 1.3cm}]{ch02_sota/figures/vgg16.pdf}
    \caption{The VGG-16 architecture~\citep{simonyan2014very}, a canonical
    \acrshort{CNN}. Stacks of $3\times3$ convolutions (blue) followed by
    max-pooling (red) progressively reduce the spatial resolution while
    increasing the number of feature maps, until a small set of fully
    connected layers (green) maps the final embedding to the $1000$ output
    classes.}
    \label{fig:vgg16}
\end{figure}


Beyond a certain depth, adding more layers
makes the network harder to optimise. ResNets~\citep{he2016deep}
resolved this with a simple architectural change. Rather than asking a
stack of layers to fit a target mapping $\mathcal{H}(x)$ directly, a
ResNet lets it fit only the \emph{residual}
$\mathcal{F}(x) = \mathcal{H}(x) - x$ and adds the input back through an
identity \emph{skip} connection, so that the block computes
$\mathcal{F}(x) + x$ (Figure~\ref{fig:resnet}). The intuition is
that if the optimal mapping is close to the identity --- as it often is
for the incremental refinements performed by successive deep layers ---
it is far easier to drive $\mathcal{F}(x)$ towards zero than to fit the
identity with a stack of non-linear layers.
 
Visualisations in \citet{li2018visualizing} show that skip connections
dramatically smooth the $\mathcal{L}$ loss landscape: the rugged, highly
non-convex surface of a deep plain network gives way to a far smoother, near-convex one that gradient descent can navigate much more easily (Figure~\ref{fig:resnet}). 

\begin{figure}[hb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.9\textwidth]{ch02_sota/figures/resnet.pdf}
    \caption{Residual networks. Effect of skip connections on the loss landscape (illustrative):  \textbf{(a)}~a deep plain network exhibits a rugged highly
    non-convex $\mathcal{L}$ surface with many poor local minima, whereas \textbf{(b)}~the
    same network with skip connections has a near-convex
    landscape much easier to optimise, following the
    visualisation of~\citet{li2018visualizing}.      
    \textbf{(c)}~A ResNet block: the two
    weight layers $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{W}^{(2)}$, with activation
    $f$, map the input through the feature maps $\mathbf{H}_1$ and
    $\mathbf{H}_2 = \mathcal{F}(x)$ (the residual), and an identity skip
    connection (red) adds the input back, so the block outputs
    $\mathcal{F}(x)+x$.}
    \label{fig:resnet}
\end{figure}

Combining the two preceding ideas yields one of the most widely used
architectures in audio processing. Taking a VGG-like stack of convolutions
and pooling as a \emph{contracting} path that compresses the input into a
low-resolution, high-channel representation, and mirroring it with a
symmetric \emph{expanding} path that upsamples back to the original
resolution, gives an \emph{encoder--decoder}. Adding skip connections in
the spirit of ResNet --- here linking each encoder layer to the decoder
layer of matching resolution, so that fine-grained detail can bypass the
bottleneck and reach the output directly --- gives the U-Net~\citep{ronneberger2015u}. Originally proposed for image segmentation, it
has become the backbone of countless source-separation and
speech-enhancement models.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{ch02_sota/figures/unet.pdf}
    \caption{The U-Net as a combination of the two preceding models: a
    VGG-like encoder--decoder (blue) with ResNet-style skip connections (red). Details are omitted for clarity.}
    \label{fig:unet}
\end{figure}

When an encoder--decoder is trained to reconstruct its own input rather
than to predict a different target, it is called an \emph{autoencoder}:
the encoder compresses the input into the low-dimensional \emph{embedding}
of Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_advent} --- here acting as a \emph{bottleneck}
or \emph{latent} --- and the decoder rebuilds the input from it, so the
network learns a compact representation with no labels at all. This
unsupervised encoder--decoder is the basis of many of the generative
models of Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_generative}.

\subsection{Temporal networks (2016--2023)}
\label{sec:bg_dl_temporal}


The architectures of the previous section map a fixed-size input to its
output in a single shot, with no notion of order. Audio, however, is
inherently \emph{sequential}: a waveform or a spectrogram is an ordered
sequence of samples or frames whose interpretation depends on context.
This section reviews three complementary ways of modelling that temporal
structure --- recurrence, autoregression, and dilated causal convolution.

\textbf{\acrfull{RNN} ---} processes a sequence one step
at a time while carrying a hidden state $h_t$ that summarises
everything seen so far. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ch02_sota/figures/rnn.pdf}
    \caption{Recurrent networks. \textbf{(a)}~A recurrent cell $f_\theta$
    maps an input $x_t$ to a hidden state $h_t$ while feeding that state
    back into the next step; unrolling the recurrence over time (right)
    reveals a deep network that shares its weights across all steps.
    \textbf{(b)}~Schematic of the plain \acrshort{RNN} cell (a single
    $\tanh$), the \acrshort{LSTM} (a cell-state highway regulated by
    forget, input and output gates), and the \acrshort{GRU} (reset and
    update gates). $\sigma$ denotes a sigmoid gate, and $\times$, $+$
    element-wise operations.}
    \label{fig:rnn}
\end{figure}

Writing $x_t$ for the input at time $t$ (e.g.\ a
single spectrogram frame), the same cell is applied at every step,
\begin{equation}
    h_t = f\!\left(\mathbf{W}_x\, x_t + \mathbf{W}_h\, h_{t-1} + b\right),
    \label{eq:rnn}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the activation (typically $\tanh$), $\mathbf{W}_x$ and
$\mathbf{W}_h$ are the input and recurrent weight matrices, and the
feedback of $h_{t-1}$ is the \emph{recurrence}. Unrolling this loop over
time (Figure~\ref{fig:rnn}a) shows that an \acrshort{RNN} is effectively a
very deep network that \emph{shares} its parameters
$\theta = \{\mathbf{W}_x, \mathbf{W}_h, b\}$ across all steps, trained by
back-propagation through time. Plain \acrshort{RNN}s struggle to retain
information over long sequences, as gradients tend to vanish or explode;
the gated \acrfull{LSTM} \citep{hochreiter1997long} and the lighter
\acrfull{GRU}~\citep{cho2014learning} mitigate this with multiplicative
gates that learn what to keep, forget and output (Figure~\ref{fig:rnn}b).
Their main drawback is practical: the recurrence in
Equation~\ref{eq:rnn} is inherently \emph{sequential} --- step $t$ cannot
be computed until $h_{t-1}$ is available --- which makes recurrent models
hard to parallelise on \acrshort{GPU}s and slow to train. They were
nonetheless the dominant sequence models of their era and remain in use,
underpinning music source-separation systems such as Open-Unmix~\citep{stoter2019openunmix} and Spleeter~\citep{hennequin2020spleeter} and
text-to-speech models such as Tacotron~2~\citep{shen2018natural}.



\textbf{Autoregressive models ---} A second strategy models a sequence by
\emph{factorising} its joint distribution into a product of conditionals,
predicting each element from the ones before it:
\begin{equation}
    p(x) = \prod_{t} p\!\left(x_t \mid x_{1}, \dots, x_{t-1}\right).
    \label{eq:autoregressive}
\end{equation}
WaveNet~\citep{van2016wavenet} was the landmark example, generating raw
audio one sample at a time with a stack of dilated causal convolutions and
reaching unprecedented synthesis quality (Figure \ref{fig:wavenet}). The same autoregressive
principle underlies today's GPT-style language models
\citep{radford2019language}: applied to the discrete tokens produced by
neural audio codecs (Section~\ref{sec:context}), it drives much of modern
generative audio \citep{borsos2023audiolm}. Autoregressive models are
expressive density estimators, but inherently sequential at inference ---
each element requires all the previous ones --- and therefore slow to
sample.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,trim={.25cm .26cm .25cm 7cm},clip]{ch02_sota/figures/wavenet.pdf}
    \caption{WaveNet model. Autoregression: each element $x_t$ is predicted
    from all the previous ones, factorising the sequence distribution as
    $p(x)=\prod_t p(x_t\mid x_{<t})$; generation proceeds one element at a
    time (dashed). A stack of \emph{dilated causal}
    convolutions: connections reach only into the past (causal) and skip
    an exponentially growing number of steps with depth (dilation
    $1,2,4,8$), so a single output (highlighted) sees a large receptive
    field --- here the entire input --- with only a few layers.}
    \label{fig:wavenet}
\end{figure}


\textbf{Temporal convolutional networks ---} A third strategy keeps the
convolutional machinery of Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_advent} but adapts it to
sequences. Temporal Concolutional Networks (TCNs) are one-dimensional \acrshort{CNN}s built from
\emph{dilated causal} convolutions stacked in residual blocks: \emph{causal}, because each output depends only on present and past inputs --- suiting
streaming and real-time use; and \emph{dilated}, because inserting gaps
between the kernel taps expands the receptive field \emph{exponentially}
with depth, so that long temporal context is captured with few layers
while remaining fully parallelisable, unlike the recurrence of
Equation~\ref{eq:rnn}. Introduced by WaveNet and characterised as a
generic sequence model by \citet{bai2018empirical}, dilated causal
convolutions are the backbone of many audio-processing systems, including
Conv-TasNet~\citep{luo2019conv} for source separation, the
waveform-domain Wave-U-Net~\citep{stoller2018wave}, and general-purpose
audio-effect removal~\citep{rice2023general}. Their efficiency and
causality made them particularly well suited to the real-time, on-device
setting. Notably, many of these temporal models ---WaveNet, Conv-TasNet and Wave-U-Net among them--- operate directly on the raw waveform, and in doing so sidestep the magnitude--phase compromise of the early spectrogram \acrshort{CNN}s of Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_advent}, which discarded the phase altogether.


\textbf{Attention and Transformers ---} The Transformer~\citep{vaswani2017attention} replaced recurrence with \emph{attention}, yielding a better
\emph{parallelisable} architecture that processes all positions of a
sequence at once rather than one step at a time --- a decisive advantage
for training on modern \acrshort{GPU}s. Its structure is
sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:transformer}. Both the input and the output are treated as sequences of discrete
\emph{tokens} --- words or sub-words for text and, in the audio domain,
short frames or the discrete codes produced by a neural codec. Each token
is first mapped to an \emph{embedding} --- the same compact, learned
vector representation introduced for \acrshort{CNN}s in
Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_advent} --- so that every token becomes a point in
a continuous space the network can operate on.

\vspace{0.8cm}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textheight]{ch02_sota/figures/transformer.pdf}
    \caption{The Transformer~\citep{vaswani2017attention}. The encoder (left) refines the input
    embeddings through stacked \emph{self-attention} and feed-forward
    sublayers, each wrapped in a residual ``Add~\& Norm'' connection (red).
    The decoder (right) generates the output autoregressively, combining
    masked self-attention with \emph{cross-attention} (green) that queries
    the encoder embeddings. This sketch is a
    simplified rendering of the original figure of
    \citet{vaswani2017attention}.}
    \label{fig:transformer}
\end{figure}

Because attention is order-agnostic, a \emph{positional
encoding} is then added to these embeddings to inject information about
each token's position in the sequence. The core operation is
\emph{self-attention}: within a sequence, every embedding is updated as a
weighted combination of all the others, so that tokens exchange
information directly regardless of how far apart they are. These attention
sublayers are stacked into blocks, each wrapped in a \emph{residual
connection} (the ``Add~\& Norm'' of Figure~\ref{fig:transformer}) in
exactly the spirit of the ResNets of Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_advent}, which
is what keeps such deep stacks trainable. A second form,
\emph{cross-attention}, lets one sequence attend to another: the decoder
queries the encoder's embeddings, which is how a generator is
\emph{conditioned} on an input.



The architecture thus splits naturally into an \emph{encoder}, which
builds contextual embeddings of the input, and a \emph{decoder}, which
generates the output autoregressively; either half can be used on its own,
as in the encoder-only BERT~\citep{devlin2019bert} for representation
learning or the decoder-only GPT-2~\citep{radford2019language} for
generation. Crucially, cross-attention does not require the two sequences
to be of the same kind: a decoder can be conditioned on embeddings from a
different \emph{modality} altogether --- text on audio, audio on text,
images on text --- which is precisely what has enabled the recent
explosion of cross-modal tasks such as text-to-audio or audio captioning.




A representative audio example is Whisper \citep{radford2022whisper}, an
encoder--decoder Transformer for speech recognition trained on hundreds of
thousands of hours of weakly labelled audio: its encoder ingests a
log-mel spectrogram and its decoder cross-attends to that representation
to produce the transcript, with a single model also performing
translation and language identification.

\subsection{Generative models (2020--2026)}
\label{sec:bg_dl_generative}

Every research objective of this thesis except the generation of audience musical noise (\textbf{RO5}) is addressed
with \emph{discriminative} models, which learn a direct mapping from an
input to a target by estimating the conditional $p(y \mid x)$ --- the
classification and regression setting of the previous sections. Only
\textbf{RO5} relies on \emph{generative} models, which instead learn the
data distribution $p(x)$ itself ---or the conditional $p(x \mid c)$ if using a conditioning input $c$ such
as a text prompt or a reference voice--- and can
\emph{sample} new, previously unseen data from it. Since the autoregressive
generative models were already introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_temporal}, this section briefly outlines the remaining
generative families at the level of detail needed to follow the rest of
the thesis.

\textbf{GANs ---} A \acrfull{GAN} from~\cite{goodfellow2014generative} casts
generation as a two-player game: a \emph{generator} maps random noise to
samples, while a \emph{discriminator} learns to tell real data from the
generator's output. The two are trained adversarially --- the generator
improving until its samples can no longer be distinguished from real ones
--- which produces sharp, realistic outputs but can be delicate to train.
In audio, \acrshort{GAN}s are the standard choice for \emph{vocoders} that
invert a mel-spectrogram back into a waveform; a vocoder of this kind,
HiFi-GAN~\citep{kong2020hifi}, underlies the voice-conversion pipeline
used in this thesis (RO5).


\textbf{Beyond supervised learning ---} All the models discussed so far
are trained with \emph{supervised} learning, which requires paired
examples $(x, y)$. \emph{Self-supervised} learning lifts this requirement
by manufacturing the target from the unlabelled data itself, through a
\emph{pretext} task. The simplest is \emph{reconstruction}: an autoencoder learns to rebuild
its input, and a \emph{denoising} autoencoder does so from a deliberately
corrupted version, forcing the embedding to capture the signal's
structure rather than copying it verbatim. A second is \emph{masking}: part of the signal, or of its latent representation, is hidden and the model must fill it in, much as
masked-language modelling does for text; HuBERT~\citep{hsu2021hubert}, for
instance, masks spans of the audio latent and predicts pre-clustered
targets at the masked positions. A third is \emph{autoregression}: predicting the next frame or token from the past is itself a supervisory
signal, which is why the autoregressive models of
Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_temporal} (WaveNet, GPT-style decoders)
are, in effect, trained in a self-supervised manner. A further,
orthogonal ingredient is the \emph{contrastive} objective, which neither
reconstructs nor predicts a value but learns \emph{by comparison}: the
representation of a \emph{positive} pair is pulled close to its context, while a set
of \emph{negative} distractors is pushed away. wav2vec~2.0~\citep{baevski2020wav2vec2} combines masking
with exactly such an objective, learning to single out the correct
quantised latent of a masked span among many distractors. Once trained,
these label-free representations can feed a small \emph{supervised}
classifier --- a \emph{semi-supervised} pipeline --- and, at scale,
they are precisely what
makes the pre-training of foundation models (Section~\ref{sec:context})
feasible.

\textbf{Discrete latent codecs ---} A further family quantises the
continuous latent of an autoencoder into a finite \emph{codebook}, turning
audio into a sequence of discrete tokens (Section~\ref{sec:context}).
Building on the discrete-codebook idea of the VQ-VAE~\citep{oord2017neural},
residual vector quantisation (RVQ) stacks several codebooks, each
quantising the residual left by the previous one, to encode the waveform
at a very low bitrate; SoundStream~\citep{zeghidour2021soundstream} and
EnCodec~\citep{defossez2022high} are the standard neural codecs of this
kind. Their training also illustrates how the pieces connect: EnCodec
combines time- and frequency-domain \emph{reconstruction} losses with an
\emph{adversarial} (\acrshort{GAN}) loss from a set of multi-scale
discriminators, bridging the discriminative models of the previous
sections with the adversarial training above. The resulting discrete codes
are exactly the tokens over which the autoregressive and Transformer
models of Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_temporal} operate, and they underpin
language-model-style audio generation~\citep{borsos2023audiolm}.

\vspace{0.8cm}

\textbf{Diffusion ---} Diffusion models~\citep{ho2020denoising} define a
fixed \emph{forward} process that gradually corrupts data into pure noise,
and learn the \emph{reverse} process that removes it step by step;
generation then starts from noise and iteratively denoises towards a clean
sample. This yields high-quality, diverse samples, but at the cost of many
sequential denoising steps. Running the process not on the raw signal but
in the compressed latent space of an autoencoder, and parametrising the
denoiser with a Transformer, gives \emph{latent diffusion} models --- the
combination behind recent text-to-audio systems such as~\cite{evans2025stable,nistal2024improving}.

\textbf{Flow matching ---}~\citep{lipman2022flow}
generalises diffusion. Instead of reversing a fixed noising process, it
learns a continuous-time \emph{velocity field} that transports a simple
noise distribution to the data distribution along a chosen probability
path, trained by directly regressing that velocity. The paths can be made
much straighter than those implied by diffusion, so high-quality samples
require far fewer integration steps, with diffusion itself recovered as a
particular case. It has quickly become a competitive and often faster
alternative for generative audio, used for instance in~\citep{mehta2024matcha}.


This section has traced the \acrshort{DNN} toolkit on which the rest of the
thesis builds: from the perceptron and the supervised training loop,
through convolutional networks, the residual
connections and encoder--decoder patterns, the extension to sequences via recurrence, autoregression,
dilated causal convolutions and attention. We presented \emph{embeddings} ---the compact learned representation that every such
architecture produces and that later models reuse --- and the
distinction between \emph{discriminative} models, which map an
input to a target, and \emph{generative} ones, which model the data
distribution and sample from it.

\textbf{However, a model is only half of the story: it is ultimately only as
good as the \emph{data} it is trained and evaluated on.} This is doubly true
in this thesis, where much of that data is not recorded but
\emph{synthesised} --- sound fields spatialised and convolved with simulated
room responses. Such synthetic data is only useful insofar as it is
\emph{ecologically valid}: faithful to the real signals the model will
encounter at deployment. And faithfulness cannot be conjured from the
generative pipeline alone; it presupposes a solid grasp of the physical
phenomena being imitated --- how sound propagates and is captured in space,
and how rooms colour it. In short, \textbf{generating good synthetic data
requires first understanding real data}. The remainder of this chapter
therefore turns from the model to the data: first the capture and spatial
representation of sound fields, and then the measurement and simulation of
\acrshort{RIR}s.

\section{Spatial audio and Ambisonics}
\label{sec:bg_spatial}

The next two chapters and the corresponding audio processing tasks --- \acrshortpl{HA}
(Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing}) and \acrshort{SE}
(Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}), \textbf{RO1} to \textbf{RO4} --- rely on spatial audio to build and
reverberate their datasets. This is most evident in \textbf{RO1},
where the evaluation benchmark for \acrshortpl{HA} is created by reproducing
complex acoustic scenes over Ambisonics. We additionally apply spatial audio concepts like
binaural-rendering and directional room simulation across
\textbf{RO2}--\textbf{RO4}. This section introduces the necessary background: first the
general paradigms of spatial audio as a whole, and then a chronological account of
Ambisonics development.

\subsection{Spatial audio paradigms}
\label{sec:bg_spatial_paradigms}
Spatial audio is the body of techniques for capturing, representing and
reproducing the directional properties of sound, so that a listener
perceives sources at their intended positions in space. Human spatial hearing relies on a small set of cues: the \acrfull{ITD} and the \acrfull{ILD} —the differences in arrival time and in level between the two ears— together with the direction-dependent spectral colouring imposed by the head, torso and outer ears. Spatial-audio systems work by evoking or preserving these cues, and their methods are commonly grouped into three paradigms: channel-based, object-based and
scene-based.

\emph{Channel-based} audio, the oldest and most widespread, stores one
signal per loudspeaker and assumes a fixed playback layout. It grew from
two-channel stereo to multichannel \emph{surround}, adding centre and
surround channels around the listener in the now-ubiquitous 5.1 and 7.1
configurations. Dolby Laboratories was
central to this evolution, popularising cinema surround with
Dolby Stereo from the mid-1970s and discrete 5.1 with Dolby Digital in the
early 1990s. In all of these formats, spatialisation is fixed at
production time through \emph{panning} --- distributing a source's energy
across the available loudspeakers according to a panning law --- so the
spatial image is correct only for the intended layout and within a small
\emph{sweet spot}.

\emph{Object-based} audio instead stores each source as a separate
\emph{audio object} together with metadata describing its position, and
defers rendering to playback time, where it is adapted to whatever
loudspeaker or headphone configuration is available. This decoupling of
content from layout underlies modern immersive formats.

\emph{Scene-based} audio sits between the two: it encodes the whole sound
field around a point, independently of any loudspeaker layout, and decodes
it to the available system at playback. Its prime example is \emph{Ambisonics}: the
sound field around a point is sampled into a series of directional
components --- the \acrfull{SH} --- of increasing angular order. Ambisonics is a \emph{complete} theory in the sense that it supports the
entire chain: a sound field can be
\emph{recorded} directly with a compact microphone array; a synthetic
source can be \emph{encoded} into that same representation by panning it
according to its direction; and the result can then be \emph{decoded} to
arbitrary loudspeaker layouts. The remainder of this section will trace its development and details these encoding and decoding steps.


Closely related to these paradigms, but concerned with
reproduction over headphones, is \emph{binaural} audio. Its
starting point is that the head and body filter incoming sound in a
direction-dependent way: most simply, the head casts an \emph{acoustic
shadow}, so that a source off to one side reaches the far ear later and
weaker than the near ear, by an amount that depends on its direction. Beyond the head, the \emph{pinna} (outer ear) and ear canal
add their own direction-dependent spectral filtering --- the peaks and
notches that, in particular, disambiguate elevation --- and, since these
depend on each person's anatomy, they vary substantially from one listener
to another. This complete filter is precisely the \acrfull{HRTF}, and is exactly what we exploit for building the datasets for \textbf{RO2}. Because measuring \acrshort{HRTF}s individually is
impractical, considerable effort goes into \emph{personalising}
them, for instance by predicting them from a 3D scan of the
listener's head and ears acquired with a depth sensor
\citep{miccini2020hrtf}. 

Finally, with the rise of Extended Reality (XR), binaural rendering of Ambisonics
has become especially attractive: because the sound field is represented
in the \acrshort{SH} domain, decoding it to the two ears reduces to
multiplying the Ambisonics signals by a fixed matrix of \acrshort{HRTF}s,
and following the listener's head rotations is itself just another matrix
multiplication.

\subsection{A chronological perspective of Ambisonics}
\label{sec:bg_ambi_timeline}

The development of Ambisonics is best understood as a gradual generalisation
of stereophony, followed by decades of refinement of its encoding and
decoding. Figure~\ref{fig:ambi_timeline} situates the milestones discussed
below in time.

\textbf{Stereo and the \acrfull{MS} technique.}
The earliest spatial-audio format, two-channel stereophony, was pioneered
by Alan Blumlein around 1930 \citep{alexander2013inventor}, who introduced
both coincident microphone techniques --- $XY$ (two coincident
cardioids) and \acrshort{MS} --- and the panning laws (such as
the sine law) still in use today. In the \acrshort{MS} technique a
forward-facing microphone, the \emph{Mid} $M$, is combined with a sideways
figure-of-eight, the \emph{Side} $S$, and the stereo loudspeaker signals are
obtained by a simple sum and difference,
\begin{equation}
    L = M + S, \qquad R = M - S,
    \label{eq:ms}
\end{equation}
so that the $M/S$ ratio controls the stereo width. The Side microphone is
the key element: being a figure-of-eight, it is positive towards the left
and negative towards the right, encoding left--right direction as a single
\emph{signed} amplitude.

\textbf{\acrshort{ITD} and \acrshort{ILD}.}
\cite{wendt1964richtungshoren} carried out careful
listening experiments on two-channel stereophony, characterising how time
and level differences between the loudspeakers map onto the perceived
direction of a phantom source. These cues reflect the classical duplex
theory of localisation: the \acrshort{ITD} dominates at low frequencies,
where diffraction keeps phase cues unambiguous, while the \acrshort{ILD}
dominates at high frequencies, where phase becomes ambiguous. 

\textbf{From \acrshort{MS} to \acrfull{FOA}.}
\citet{gerzon1973periphony} took the step from two-channel stereo
to a complete description of the sound field, in what he called
\emph{periphony} and we now know as First-Order Ambisonics. The idea
generalises the \acrshort{MS} pair: the Side figure-of-eight is just one
first-order directional component, so capturing the \emph{full} first-order
field requires the omnidirectional pressure --- the Mid, when it is an
omni --- together with \emph{three} orthogonal figure-of-eight components
aligned with the front--back, left--right and up--down axes. These four
signals are the \emph{B-format} channels $\mathbf{b} = [\,W, X, Y, Z\,]^\top$.

Mathematically, this is the expansion of the sound field around a point
into \acrfull{SH}, truncated at first order \citep{zotter2019ambisonics}.
The \acrshort{SH} are a set of functions on the sphere of increasing
angular \emph{order} $\ell$ (Figure~\ref{fig:spherical_harmonics}): order
$\ell = 0$ is a single omnidirectional function (the pressure $W$), order
$\ell = 1$ adds the three dipoles $X, Y, Z$, and each higher order
contributes $2\ell + 1$ functions of finer angular detail, used by
\acrfull{HOA}. First-Order Ambisonics keeps orders $\ell \le 1$, the four
B-format channels; in this work we use Ambisonics and \acrshort{SH}
interchangeably.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth,trim={.25cm .25cm .75cm 1.25cm},clip]{ch02_sota/figures/spherical_harmonics.pdf}
    \caption{The real spherical harmonics up to order $\ell = 3$, the basis
    functions of the Ambisonics sound-field representation.  Orders $\ell \le 1$ 
    form \acrshort{FOA}, i.e.\ the B-format; each additional order adds
    $2\ell + 1$ components of finer angular resolution, used by
    \acrshort{HOA}.}
    \label{fig:spherical_harmonics}
\end{figure}

\emph{Encoding.} A mono source $s$ arriving from azimuth $\theta$ and
elevation $\varphi$ is panned into B-format by weighting it with the
spherical harmonics evaluated at that direction. In the now-standard
\emph{ACN} channel ordering with \emph{SN3D} normalisation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{b} = [\,W, Y, Z, X\,]^\top = s\,\mathbf{y}(\theta,\varphi),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{y}(\theta,\varphi) =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 \\ \sin\theta\cos\varphi \\ \sin\varphi \\ \cos\theta\cos\varphi
    \end{bmatrix},
    \label{eq:foa_encode}
\end{equation}
so that $W = s$ carries the pressure while $X, Y, Z$ carry the projection
of the source onto the three axes; a full sound field is the superposition
of all such contributions. (The older FuMa B-format orders the channels
$[\,W, X, Y, Z\,]$ and scales $W$ by $1/\sqrt{2}$; the conventions differ
only by a permutation and constant factors.)


\emph{A-format versus B-format.} The same B-format can be captured
acoustically. Ideally it would be recorded with one coincident
omnidirectional microphone (for $W$) and three coincident figure-of-eight
microphones aligned with the axes (for $X, Y, Z$). In practice this is
approximated by a compact tetrahedral array of four cardioid capsules,
whose raw signals form the \emph{A-format}
$\mathbf{a} = [\,a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4\,]^\top$; B-format is then obtained
through a fixed linear transform --- sums yield the omni $W$, differences
yield the dipoles --- $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{a}$, followed by a
frequency correction for the finite capsule spacing. A-format is thus what
the microphone records, whereas B-format is the layout-independent
representation we work with. A-format is encountered only when physically
recording with an Ambisonics microphone, and even then only as an internal
intermediate; a large share of today's Ambisonics content is instead
\emph{encoded} synthetically --- panned, simulated, or rendered --- straight into B-format. We introduce
it here mainly to make the step from \acrshort{MS} recording to Ambisonics
concrete.



\emph{Decoding.} To play a B-format signal over $L$ loudspeakers at unit
directions $\mathbf{n}_l = (\cos\theta_l\cos\varphi_l,\,
\sin\theta_l\cos\varphi_l,\, \sin\varphi_l)$, each feed is obtained as a
\emph{virtual microphone} pointing towards that loudspeaker,
\begin{equation}
    p_l = a_0\,W + a_1\,\big(\mathbf{n}_l \cdot [\,X, Y, Z\,]^\top\big),
    \label{eq:foa_decode}
\end{equation}
where $(a_0, a_1)$ set the directivity of the virtual first-order
microphone (e.g.\ a cardioid), so that $p_l$ predominantly picks up energy
from the speaker's direction; stacking the $L$ feeds gives the matrix form
$\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{b}$. This also makes the \acrshort{MS}
analogy precise: keeping only $W$ and $Y$ and decoding to two opposing
cardioids ($\mathbf{n}_{L} = (0,1,0)$, $\mathbf{n}_{R} = (0,-1,0)$) recovers
exactly $L = W + Y$ and $R = W - Y$, i.e.\ Equation~\ref{eq:ms}. B-format is
therefore the \acrshort{MS} pair completed: adding the front--back dipole $X$
and the vertical dipole $Z$ turns a fixed two-channel stereo image into a
rotatable, layout-independent sound field.

The same Gerzon will later introduce in 1992 the energy
and velocity localisation vectors $r_E$ and $r_V$ commonly used to
summarise these cues \citep{gerzon1992general},
$r_E$ in particular becoming a standard descriptor of directional
concentration: $\|r_E\| = 0$ indicates no directionality and
$\|r_E\| = 1$ a spatial Dirac.


\textbf{A parallel line: amplitude panning.}
Developed independently of, and in parallel to, Ambisonics,
\citet{pulkki1997virtual} introduced \acrfull{VBAP}, which reproduces a
source by decomposing space into loudspeaker pairs or triplets and panning
within one. It is robust (only a few loudspeakers are active at a time) and
easy to adapt to irregular layouts, but --- unlike Ambisonics --- it is not
a complete theory (recording is not possible), and the apparent source size
and coloration vary with position. \citet{pulkki1999uniform} mitigates the
latter by spreading each source across a pair of auxiliary directions at
$r_E \pm \alpha$. Although a distinct paradigm, \acrshort{VBAP} later
re-enters the Ambisonics pipeline as the loudspeaker-panning stage of the
AllRAD decoder (below).

\textbf{Refining encoding and decoding.}
Within Ambisonics, subsequent work largely refines how the sound field is
encoded and, especially, decoded --- including to binaural. In
\citet{jot1999comparative}, techniques for combining binaural audio and
Ambisonics are discussed: a basic binaural render is obtained by decoding
Ambisonics to a virtual, ideally placed loudspeaker layout and convolving
each virtual loudspeaker with its \acrfull{HRIR} pair --- especially
practical when real-time rotations must follow a head-tracking device.


\textbf{\acrfull{HOA}}. In \citet{daniel2000representation}, \acrshort{FOA} is expanded to
\acrshort{HOA} for regular layouts, trading the constant source size and
smooth panning of low orders for a larger sweet spot and sharper
localisation, at the cost of as many loudspeakers as channels. 

The Ambisonics signal becomes a vector $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)^2}$
collecting all spherical harmonics up to order $N$, and encoding a source
keeps the same form,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{b} = s\,\mathbf{y}_N(\theta,\varphi), \qquad
    \mathbf{y}_N(\theta,\varphi)=\big[\,Y_0^{0},\,Y_1^{-1},\,Y_1^{0},\,
    Y_1^{1},\,\dots,\,Y_N^{N}\,\big]^{\!\top}\!(\theta,\varphi),
    \label{eq:hoa_encode}
\end{equation}
with the $(N+1)^2$ real spherical harmonics $Y_\ell^{m}$ evaluated at the
source direction (\acrshort{FOA} is the case $N=1$). Decoding to $L$
loudspeakers is likewise a single linear map,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{b}, \qquad
    \mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times(N+1)^2},
    \label{eq:hoa_decode}
\end{equation}

whose decoding matrix $\mathbf{D}$ is built from the loudspeaker directions
(the basic decoder takes $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{Y}^{+}$, the pseudo-inverse of
the spherical-harmonic sampling matrix). Binaural rendering is simply
the case $L=2$, where the rows of $\mathbf{D}$ are the left- and right-ear
\acrshort{HRTF}s expressed in the \acrshort{SH} domain. This basic decoding works well only at low frequencies;
Daniel therefore robustifies the decoder by weighting the \acrshort{SH}s so
that the secondary lobes of the virtual microphones are attenuated
($\mathrm{max}\,r_E$) or suppressed (the in-phase decoder). Tapering the \acrshort{SH} orders in this way is the
angular counterpart of \emph{windowing} a signal before a Fourier
analysis: smoothing a truncated expansion lowers its side lobes (here, the
off-axis leakage of the virtual microphones) at the price of a wider main
lobe (a loss of angular resolution).

In \citet{avni2010sound}, \acrshort{HRTF}s are represented in the
\acrshort{SH} domain, enabling interpolation between their discrete
measured directions, and in \citet{zotter2012all} AllRAD provides
a physics-based \acrshort{HOA} decoder for irregular layouts by first
decoding to an ideal virtual layout and then rendering those signals with the available speaker triplets through
\acrshort{VBAP}. We use AllRAD in Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing}.

\vspace{0.8cm}

\textbf{Ambisonics to Binaural}. The remaining developments concern improving binaural rendering of 
limited-order Ambisonics. \citet{bernschutz2013spherical} address low-frequency group
delay in \acrshort{HRIR} databases by replacing the low band with a
synthetic spherical model called \acrfull{LFE}, while
\citet{bernschutz2014binaural} show that pairing high-order \acrshort{HRTF}s
with lower-order soundfields causes severe losses in the 
high frequencies in a frequency-dependent way; \citet{ben2017spectral} compensate the resulting
low-pass effect of \acrshort{SH} truncation with a global Spectral
Equalisation (SpecEQ) filter. \citet{zaunschirm2018binaural} adapt the
\acrshort{HRIR}s for direct low-order rendering by removing the
\acrshort{ITD} linear-phase component and matching diffuse-field covariances
(Time Alignment and diffuseness Constraint, TAC), and
\citet{schorkhuber2018binaural} propose \acrfull{MagLS}, which in the high
frequencies optimises the magnitudes alone, disregarding phase. In parallel,
\citet{armstrong2018bi} introduce a bi-radial render with two sets of
virtual loudspeakers, one centred at each ear, gaining up to two Ambisonic
orders of perceptual quality, and \citet{hold2019improving} reduce
angle-dependent artefacts by also tapering the \acrshort{SH}s.
Finally, \citet{ben2021binaural} time-align the \acrshort{HRIR}s with
respect to each ear for direct bilateral rendering, and these threads are
combined in \citet{engel2021improving}, the current state of the art:
\acrfull{BiMagLS}, which ear-aligns the \acrshort{HRTF}s, fits the
low-frequency \acrshort{SH}-\acrshort{HRTF}s with an L2 approximation of the
higher-order version, and disregards phase at high frequencies as in
\acrshort{MagLS}. We use \acrshort{BiMagLS} in Chapters~\ref{ch03_hearing} and~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}.

\textbf{Beyond this scope.}
Several further directions, left out of the timeline above because they lie
outside the scope of this thesis, are nonetheless worth signposting: \acrshort{HOA} coding \citep{hold2023compression}, \acrshort{DNN}-based microphone-to-Ambisonics encoding \citep{heikkinen2025gen}, continuous representations of the sound
field, or of the room impulse responses themselves, so that the response at
unmeasured positions can be interpolated from only a sparse set of probes \citep{luo2022learning, masuyama2025physics, erdem2026sf, baek2025av} or spatial audio generation from vision, producing Ambisonics soundtracks directly from
360\textdegree{} video \citep{morgado2018self, liu2025omniaudio}. 

\section{Room impulse responses}
\label{sec:bg_rir}
\acrshort{RIR}s are recurrent in this thesis:
in Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing}, to assemble the
\acrshort{HRTF}s of \acrshortpl{HA}; in Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs} used to generate a \acrshort{SE} dataset; and in Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss} to measure a
dataset of concert-hall responses for live \acrshort{MSS}. We devote this section to defining and to reviewing \acrshort{RIR}s and how to simulate them.


Sound propagation from a source to a receiver in a room is, to a good
approximation, a linear time-invariant system. Its \acrfull{RIR} $h(t)$ is the
signal captured at the receiver when the source emits an ideal impulse ---
the Dirac delta $\delta(t)$ --- and it characterises that source--receiver
path completely: the signal $y(t)$ recorded for any emitted signal $s(t)$
is the convolution
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = (s * h)(t)
         = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s(\tau)\,h(t-\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau .
    \label{eq:rir_convolution}
\end{equation}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:rir}, a \acrshort{RIR} is conventionally
split into three regions. The \emph{direct sound} is the first arrival,
travelling straight from source to receiver. It is followed by the
\emph{early reflections}, a sparse set of strong, temporally distinct
reflections off the nearest boundaries, whose timing and direction shape the
perceived location, width and timbre of the source. As time passes the
reflections grow exponentially denser until they can no longer be resolved
individually, forming the diffuse \emph{late reverberation} (or
\emph{tail}), which decays approximately exponentially and is described
statistically rather than reflection by reflection --- most commonly through
the reverberation time $T_{60}$, the time its energy takes to decay by
60\,dB.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.75\columnwidth]{ch02_sota/figures/rir.pdf}
    \caption{A typical room impulse response and its three regions: the
    direct sound, the early reflections, and the late reverberation tail.}
    \label{fig:rir}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Measuring RIRs with the exponential sweep}
\label{sec:bg_rir_sweep}


In principle, a \acrshort{RIR} could be obtained by exciting the room acoustically with a
physical impulse --- like a pistol shot or a bursting balloon --- and recording
the result, but such sources are poorly repeatable, difficult to equalise
and offer a limited \acrfull{SNR}. 

In practice, the emitted signal $s(t)$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:rir_convolution} is an excitation reproduced by the
\emph{source} loudspeaker, and $y(t)$ is what a measurement
microphone at the \emph{receiver} position records; the goal is to recover $h(t)$
from this known input and recorded output. For the measured response to
reflect the room rather than the measurement chain, both transducers are
traditionally chosen to be as neutral and direction-independent as
possible: the microphone is an omnidirectional capsule with a flat magnitude
and linear-phase response, and the source approximates an ideal point
emitter radiating uniformly in all directions --- classically a dodecahedron
loudspeaker, whose twelve drivers on the faces of the solid yield
near-omnidirectional coverage. 

A \acrshort{RIR} can also be estimated
from \emph{continuous noise} excitation, as routinely done in live sound
with dual-channel Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analysers that recover the transfer
function between the noise feeding the loudspeakers and a measurement
microphone in the room. The now-standard alternative, however, is the
Exponential Swept-Sine method of \citet{farina2000simultaneous}, in which the room is
excited with a sinusoid $x(t)$ whose instantaneous frequency rises exponentially
from $f_1$ to $f_2$ over a duration $T$,

\begin{equation}
    x(t) = \sin\!\left[ \frac{2\pi f_1 T}{\ln(f_2/f_1)}
           \left( e^{\,\frac{t}{T}\ln(f_2/f_1)} - 1 \right) \right],
           \qquad 0 \le t \le T .
    \label{eq:ess}
\end{equation}

The \acrshort{RIR} is recovered by \emph{deconvolution} with an inverse
filter $f(t)$ designed so that $x * f = \delta$. This filter is the
time-reversed sweep, $x(T-t)$, weighted by an exponentially decaying
envelope that whitens the sweep's pink spectrum,
\begin{equation}
    f(t) \;\propto\; x(T-t)\,\left(\frac{f_1}{f_2}\right)^{\!t/T},
    \label{eq:ess_inverse}
\end{equation}
up to a normalisation that sets $x * f = \delta$. Convolving the recorded
signal $y(t)$ with it,
\begin{equation}
    h(t) = (y * f)(t),
    \label{eq:ess_deconv}
\end{equation}
collapses the sweep back to an impulse and returns the \acrshort{RIR}. Applying a time window to $h(t)$ is itself a versatile
analysis tool, since cropping the \acrshort{RIR} in time selects which part
of the source--receiver interaction is retained. Windowing out everything
before $t=0$ discards the harmonic-distortion responses that the
ESS maps to negative times. Keeping only a short
segment after the direct sound, ending before the first early reflection,
yields a \emph{quasi-anechoic} response that captures the magnitude and
phase of the measurement chain itself --- loudspeaker, microphone and
direct path --- free of the room. Extending the window to include the early
reflections brings in the strongly \emph{position-dependent} part of the
response, which varies markedly with source and receiver placement within
the room. The late reverberation, by contrast, is diffuse and statistically
similar throughout the room.


\subsection{Room impulse response simulation}
\label{sec:bg_rir_ism}

The most straightforward way to simulate a \acrshort{RIR} is the \acrfull{ISM}
\citep{allen1979image}. It rests on a simple geometric observation: a
specular reflection off a flat wall is indistinguishable, at the receiver,
from the direct sound of a \emph{mirror image} of the source, placed behind
the wall at the same perpendicular distance (Figure~\ref{fig:ism}). Each
boundary thus replaces a bounce by a virtual \emph{image source}, and the
reflected path is recovered as the straight line from that image to the
receiver. Reflections off several walls are handled by mirroring
recursively --- an image reflected again across another wall is a
second-order image, and so on --- so that an order-$N$ reflection
corresponds to an image mirrored $N$ times; for a rectangular (shoebox)
room these images form a regular lattice of mirrored copies of the room.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.85\columnwidth]{ch02_sota/figures/ism_first_order.pdf}
    \caption{The \acrshort{ISM}, illustrated to first order. Receiver $R$, source $S$, \emph{image source} $S'$. The reflected path
    $S \rightarrow P \rightarrow R$ then coincides with the straight line from
    $S'$ to $R$, so the reflection reduces to a delayed, attenuated copy of the
    direct sound.}
    \label{fig:ism}
\end{figure}
Applying the method yields, for
every reflection $i$, the coordinates of its image source and its order
$N_i$, which is all that is needed to render the response. The
image-to-receiver distance $d_i$ fixes both the arrival time
$\tau_i = d_i/c$ (with $c$ the speed of sound) and the attenuation due to
spherical spreading, which decays the pressure as $1/d_i$. The energy lost
at each bounce is set by the walls' absorption coefficient $\alpha$ (the
absorbed fraction of incident energy), or equivalently by the amplitude
reflection coefficient $\beta = \sqrt{1-\alpha}$; a path of order $N_i$ has
therefore been attenuated by the product of the $\beta$ of the walls it
visited, i.e.\ $\beta^{N_i}$ for uniform boundaries. The \acrshort{RIR} is
then the sum of one delayed, attenuated impulse per image,
\begin{equation}
    h(t) = \sum_{i} \frac{\beta^{N_i}}{d_i}\,
           \delta\!\left(t - \frac{d_i}{c}\right).
    \label{eq:ism}
\end{equation}

In practice the absorption is rarely known from material data; one usually
targets a desired reverberation time instead. The two are linked by
Sabine's equation \citep{sabine2015collected}, $T_{60} = 0.161\,V/A$, with $V$ the room volume and
$A=\sum_k S_k \alpha_k$ the total absorption (surface area times absorption,
summed over boundaries). For uniform walls this inverts to
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \frac{0.161\,V}{T_{60}\,S_\mathrm{tot}}, \qquad
    \beta=\sqrt{1-\alpha},
    \label{eq:sabine}
\end{equation}
so a single \emph{target} $T_{60}$ determines the $\beta$ used in
Equation~\ref{eq:ism}.

The \acrshort{ISM} is exact for the
specular reflections of a rigid-walled shoebox, which makes it an efficient
and ubiquitous way to render the direct sound and early reflections. Some of
its idealisations can be relaxed within the method itself: the real,
frequency- and angle-dependent wall impedance can be modelled by replacing
the scalar $\beta$ with per-band reflection filters; source and receiver
directivity can be applied per
image; and air absorption can be added as a distance-dependent filter. A
subtler artefact is that, with a real positive $\beta$ and impulses placed
at integer samples, every reflection is positive, so the simulated
\acrshort{RIR} is biased towards positive values, while measured
responses are essentially zero-mean and symmetric about the time axis. This
is sometimes corrected by rendering each arrival with a band-limited fractional-delay
filter --- a windowed sinc, whose side lobes restore the negative
excursions \citep{peterson1986simulating}.

Finally, the regular-lattice construction is restricted to shoebox
geometries, and the number of images grows cubically with order, so the
method becomes impractical precisely where the late, diffuse reverberation
begins. It is therefore sometimes truncated at low order and paired with a
statistical model of the tail \citep{lehmann2009diffuse}.

Other assumptions require a different paradigm altogether. The
\acrshort{ISM} models only specular reflections, ignoring the diffuse
\emph{scattering} of rough surfaces and the \emph{diffraction} around edges
and obstacles; both call for ray- or beam-tracing with scattering
coefficients, radiosity, or edge-diffraction models \citep{savioja2015overview}. Being a geometrical approximation, it also misses the modal behaviour and wave
interference that dominate at low frequencies, which only wave-based solvers
capture:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ray tracing.} Rather than enumerating image sources, ray tracing
shoots a large number of rays from the source and follows
them as they bounce around the room, tallying those that reach a receiver
volume. Its key advantage over the \acrshort{ISM} is that reflections don't need to be ideally specular: at each surface a ray can be scattered into a
random direction according to the boundary's scattering coefficient, so the
diffuse reflections that the \acrshort{ISM} ignores are captured through
Monte-Carlo sampling. The cost is statistical: accuracy improves only as
more rays are traced, and very late or weak paths are noisy.

\item \textbf{Ray-radiosity.} Radiosity-based methods are instead
\emph{surface}-based. The boundaries are subdivided into patches, and the
acoustic energy exchanged between every pair of patches is computed under
the assumption of (ideally diffuse) reflection, yielding the time-dependent
energy decay across the room without tracking individual paths. They model
diffuse reverberation efficiently but, by construction, do not represent
specular reflections or phase.

\item \textbf{Wave-based methods.} At the opposite end, wave-based solvers
discretise and solve the acoustic wave equation directly --- through
finite-difference time-domain, finite-element or boundary-element
formulations --- and therefore reproduce wave phenomena that geometrical
methods omit, such as modal behaviour, interference and diffraction. They
are more accurate at low frequencies compared to \acrshort{ISM}, but their cost grows
steeply with frequency, which
restricts them in practice to the lower part of the spectrum.

\item \textbf{Hybrid methods.} Since no single technique is accurate and
affordable across the whole spectrum and the whole response, practical
simulators are usually \emph{hybrid}, assigning each regime to the method
that suits it best: wave-based solvers for the low frequencies, where modes
dominate; and a path-based method such as the \acrshort{ISM} for the
high-frequency early part, where specular reflections matter and timing is
critical \citep{tang2022gwa}.

\item \textbf{\acrshort{DNN}-based methods.} More recently, \acrshortpl{DNN} generate \acrshort{RIR}s directly, conditioned on a 3D scene mesh \citep{ratnarajah2022mesh2ir} or on perceptual descriptors via diffusion \citep{arellano2025room}. Classical solvers have also been accelerated on GPU \citep{diaz2021gpurir}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Survey of publicly-available RIR datasets}
\label{sec:bg_rir_datasets}

A number of \acrshort{RIR} datasets are publicly available; Table~\ref{tab:rir_datasets}
summarises them. Two axes are worth tracking throughout, as they define our
research gap: the sampling rate and the number of \emph{distinct} rooms, since
most datasets cover either very few rooms or only low sampling rates. We
review the measured datasets first and then the simulated ones.

\textbf{Real ---}~RWCP-SSD~\citep{nakamura2000acoustical} is one of the earliest resources, with 143
microphone-array responses at 48\,kHz across 14 real offices and rooms for
distant-speech research. The Aachen Impulse Response database
(AIR)~\citep{jeub2009binaural} provides 344 binaural responses at 48\,kHz
measured with a dummy head across just 5 rooms, one of them a church, and was
originally motivated by hearing-aid and dereverberation studies. C4DM
\citep{stewart2010database} offers 468 mono and first-order Ambisonic
(\acrshort{FOA}) responses at a high 96\,kHz, but only in 3 large London
spaces, while OpenAIR\footnote{\url{https://www.openair.hosted.york.ac.uk/}}
is a community \acrshort{FOA} library spanning some 46 real, often heritage or
``extreme'' environments (cathedrals, tunnels) at 48\,kHz, without a fixed
protocol or response count.

Several databases were conceived as speech-processing benchmarks, and are
correspondingly small in rooms and sampling rate. Take, for example, the REVERB
challenge database~\citep{kinoshita2013reverb}, which offers microphone-array responses in
only 3 room sizes at 16\,kHz for dereverberation, \acrshort{ASR} and
\acrshort{SE}; MIRD~\citep{hadad2014multichannel} captures 234
eight-microphone-array responses at 48\,kHz in a single room, at three
different $T_{60}$ values and several array spacings; and the ACE
corpus~\citep{eaton2015ace} records 7 rooms at 48\,kHz with a range of devices
(laptop, phone, and an em32 Eigenmike, hence up to higher-order Ambisonics)
for blind $T_{60}$ and Direct-to-Reverberant Ratio (DRR) estimation. The MIT IR
survey~\citep{traer2016statistics} instead maximises room diversity, with 271
mono responses each measured in a distinct everyday space, but at 44.1\,kHz
and a single response per room, while BUT ReverbDB~\citep{szoke2019building}
adds retransmitted speech and noise with about 1.3k responses across 9 real
rooms, at 16\,kHz.

A more recent trend targets \emph{spatial} and densely sampled measurements,
typically within a single room. TAU-SRIR~\citep{politis2020dataset} provides
114 \acrshort{FOA} and tetrahedral-array responses at 24\,kHz across 9
classrooms, gyms and halls, and underpins the DCASE sound-event localisation
benchmarks. dEchorate~\citep{carlo2021dechorate} annotates the timings of
early reflections and the tail, together with the 3D positions of the
corresponding image sources, with about 1.8k responses at 48\,kHz in a single
room; MeshRIR~\citep{koyama2021meshrir} densely samples one room on a
source-and-receiver grid (4.4k responses at 48\,kHz) for sound-field
reconstruction; Motus~\citep{gotz2021dataset} measures 3.3k higher-order
Ambisonics responses at 48\,kHz, again in a single room, across 830 furniture
configurations; and Arni\footnote{\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.6985104}}
records over 132k responses at 44.1\,kHz within one variable-acoustics room,
spanning 5342 configurations of movable absorptive panels.

The most recent measured datasets push count and automation further, but
rarely the number of rooms. SoundCam~\citep{wang2023soundcam} records 5k
responses at 48\,kHz across 3 rooms with a moving human present, for tracking
people from room acoustics; MIRACLE~\citep{kujawski2024miracle} contributes a
large 856k responses at 48\,kHz from a planar 64-microphone array on dense
source grids, but in a single anechoic chamber (optionally with a reflecting
panel); and GTU-RIR~\citep{pekmezci2024evaluation} collects around 15k
responses at 44.1\,kHz over 11 rooms with a semi-automated rig.
HOMULA-RIR~\citep{miotello2024homula} models a teleconference in a single
seminar room using higher-order microphones and a linear array (52 responses
at 48\,kHz), and MP-RIR~\citep{friede2024multi} samples one complex-shaped
room on a dense 3D grid with a robot-automated rig (68.7k responses at
48\,kHz). FLAIR~\citep{sundstrom2025bayesian} pairs 270 responses at 48\,kHz
in a single room with laser-measured 3D point clouds of the geometry;
SRIRACHA~\citep{pelling2025sriracha} provides 2.65M responses at 48\,kHz as an
interpolation benchmark, yet within a single shoebox with varying absorption;
and UPV\_RIR\_DB~\citep{garciagamborino2026upv} releases 166 multichannel
responses at 48\,kHz across 3 rooms with hierarchical metadata and a per-pair
spatial grid. Finally, OpenSLR28~\citep{ko2017study} is not a single campaign
but the widely used augmentation \emph{bundle} that aggregates real responses
from RWCP, REVERB and AIR with simulated responses and point-source noises,
all distributed at 16\,kHz.

\textbf{Synthetic ---}~Obtaining room diversity at scale by measuring is impractical, which motivates simulating synthetic \acrshort{RIR}s. BIRD~\citep{grondin2020bird} provides 100k stereo responses at 16\,kHz
generated with the \acrshort{ISM} across as many shoebox rooms; the spacing
between the two microphones of each A/B pair is drawn uniformly as
$\sim \mathcal{U}(0.1, 0.3)\,\text{m}$, although the microphone directivity is
left unspecified. SoundSpaces~\citep{chen2022soundspaces} renders about 16M
responses at 16\,kHz for the Matterport3D meshes and 44.1\,kHz for the Replica subset, over 101 scanned indoor scenes with a bidirectional
path-tracing simulator (continuous rendering in version~2.0); GWA~\citep{tang2022gwa} computes around 2M
mono responses at 48\,kHz over some 18.9k furnished rooms, using a hybrid
simulator that couples a finite-difference time-domain wave solver (low
frequencies) with ray tracing (high frequencies) and semantic
acoustic-material assignment. Lastly, ReverbFX~\citep{richter2025reverbfx}
departs from physical rooms altogether, deriving 1.85k stereo responses at
48\,kHz from professional reverb effect plugins (convolution-based and
algorithmic) to study singing-voice dereverberation under artificial
reverberation.

Table~\ref{tab:rir_datasets} reveals a consistent trade-off along our
two axes. Measured datasets are mostly single-room campaigns
(dEchorate, MeshRIR, Motus, Arni, MIRACLE, HOMULA-RIR, MP-RIR, FLAIR and
SRIRACHA), and even the broadest rarely exceed a dozen rooms (14 for
RWCP-SSD, 11 for GTU-RIR, 9 for BUT ReverbDB and TAU-SRIR); only the MIT
survey spans many rooms (271). Synthetic datasets, by contrast, can cover hundreds or thousands of rooms, but
at the cost of lower sampling rates. BIRD covers 100k shoebox rooms and the Matterport3D subset of
SoundSpaces 85 scanned houses, both at 16\,kHz. The higher-rate options are
in turn restricted in scene count --- the Replica subset of
SoundSpaces reaches 44.1\,kHz but spans only 18 scenes --- except for GWA which
attains 48\,kHz over some 18.9k rooms. The augmentation bundle most commonly used
for speech enhancement, OpenSLR28 --- the default choice in the DNS
challenges --- is likewise dated and limited to 16\,kHz.

These limitations frame the gaps addressed in the following chapters: no
dataset targets \acrlongpl{HA} (Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing}); no
purpose-built, higher-fidelity synthetic \acrshort{RIR}s exist for
\acrshort{SE} beyond GWA and the dated 16\,kHz bundles
(Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}); and no \emph{concert-hall} or live-music-venue
dataset is available at the scale and sampling rate required for source
separation of live recordings (Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss}).
