\chapter{Extending Music Source Separation to Live Recordings with Audience Noise and Concert-Venue Impulse Responses}
\chaptermark{MSS from Live Recordings}
\label{ch05_mss}

The two previous chapters improve the ecological validity of training data for
speech; this final chapter brings the same idea to music, in line with the
research interests of the \acrfull{MTG}, by proposing a new task: adapting
existing \acrfull{MSS} datasets to a new use case, live recordings made from the
audience.

\acrshort{MSS} --- decomposing a musical mixture into its constituent sources,
conventionally \emph{vocals}, \emph{drums}, \emph{bass} and \emph{other} --- has
advanced rapidly since the standardisation of the task
\citep{rafii2019musdbhq, stoter2019openunmix}, with successive architectures
\citep{rouard2023htdemucs, luo2023bsrnn, tong2024scnet} and community
challenges \citep{fabbro2024sdx23, mitsufuji2022music} steadily improving
separation quality. Yet almost all of this progress rests on a single
assumption: that the sources mix \emph{linearly}, as they do in the studio. That
assumption breaks down for \emph{live} music recorded from the audience --- the
kind of phone recording anyone attending a concert might make --- where three
factors reshape the signal before it reaches the microphone: the venue
acoustics, the response of the \acrfull{PA} loudspeaker system, and the noise of
the crowd itself. Applied to such recordings, studio-trained models degrade
sharply, and the few prior works in live settings are confined either to a
specific tradition \citep{plaja-carnatic_separation} or to music enhancement without
separation \citep{kandpal2022musicenhancement}.

The situation mirrors one already met in speech separation, where the WHAM!
\citep{wichern2019wham} and WHAMR! \citep{maciejewski2020whamr} datasets bridged
an analogous gap by augmenting the clean WSJ0-2mix corpus with ambient noise and
reverberation. We follow the same idea for music: we \emph{formulate} a new \acrfull{MIR}
task, which we call \emph{Live} \acrshort{MSS} --- the separation of music recorded from within a
live audience, where venue acoustics, the \acrshort{PA} system and crowd noise
break the linear-mixing assumption of studio \acrshort{MSS}, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:mss_paradigm}. Rather than relying on the scarce and
hard-to-annotate real live recordings, we \emph{synthesise} live conditions on
top of existing studio datasets --- exactly the ecological-validity-through-synthesis
strategy of the rest of this thesis.

Importantly, our use of \emph{live} refers to the \emph{acoustic conditions} of
music captured from within a live audience --- venue reverberation,
\acrshort{PA} colouration and crowd noise --- and \emph{not} to latency. It is
therefore unrelated to the parallel line of work on \emph{real-time}, low-latency
\acrshort{MSS} for streaming and on-stage use \citep{venkatesh2024real}, where
\emph{live} instead denotes low-latency processing: our task concerns
\emph{where} the recording was made, not \emph{how fast} it is separated, and
applies whether separation is performed online or offline.

Concretely, and targeting \emph{audience-side} recordings degraded by crowd
noise and venue acoustics, this chapter contributes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CrowdioSet}, an \emph{audience} noise dataset: a curated
    selection of real ambience and event recordings from Freesound
    \citep{anastasopoulou2025freesound} (\textbf{RO6}), together with a
    generative pipeline that produces synthetic \emph{sing-alongs} by
    zero-shot singing-voice conversion of the vocals in existing datasets
    (\textbf{RO5});
    \item \textbf{PaRIRset}, a stereo \acrshort{RIR} dataset measured across
    $40$ professional concert venues with a microphone array, the first captured
    in real venues through their \acrshort{PA} systems (\textbf{RO7}); and
    \item a \emph{Live} \acrshort{MSS} baseline, obtained by re-training the
    open-source SCNet model \citep{tong2024scnet} on studio data augmented with
    both datasets;
    \item the formulation of \emph{Live} \acrshort{MSS} as a new \acrshort{MIR}
    task, together with a reproducible recipe for synthesising its training and
    evaluation data from studio corpora;
    \item We release the datasets, code, model weights and audio examples publicly.
\end{itemize}

\section{Music source separation}
\label{sec:sota_mss}

Having motivated the task, we now review the models and data that define it.
Modern separators fall into three broad families (Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl}):
waveform-domain models such as the Demucs line \citep{rouard2023htdemucs},
spectrogram-domain models such as the Band-Split RNN \citep{luo2023bsrnn}, and
hybrid models that operate in both, such as SCNet \citep{tong2024scnet} --- the
backbone we adopt in this chapter for its strong performance and open-source
availability. 


Around this core, recent work has broadened the task in several
directions: generative approaches to separation \citep{plaja2025generating},
lightweight models for real-time or on-device use
\citep{hung2025moises, venkatesh2024real}, revisited evaluation metrics that
better track perception \citep{jaffe2025musical, sheridan2025perceptual}, and a
move beyond the fixed \emph{vocals}/\emph{drums}/\emph{bass}/\emph{other}
taxonomy towards universal \citep{postolache2023adversarial} and text-prompted
separation \citep{shi2025samaudio}, the latter of which we include as a
baseline. 

For all this progress, the models are almost invariably trained and evaluated on
\emph{studio} material, where the stems mix linearly and cleanly. Public corpora
reflect this: MUSDB18-HQ \citep{rafii2019musdbhq} and the larger MOISESDB
\citep{pereira2023moisesdb} both provide clean, close-miked studio stems, with no
venue acoustics, no loudspeaker colouration and no audience. A parallel
diagnosis motivates the recent Music Source Restoration task
\citep{zang2025music}, which observes that \acrshort{MSS} treats a mixture as a
plain sum of sources, ignoring the equalisation, compression and reverberation
applied during music \emph{production}, and instead models it as a degraded sum
of degraded sources to be recovered. Live music, and in particular the
audience-side recordings that motivate this chapter, violates the linear-mixing
assumption in a different way, through the acoustics and noise of the venue
rather than the studio production chain. The few works that venture there remain
narrow: \citet{plaja-carnatic_separation} addresses separation for Carnatic
music, and \citet{kandpal2022musicenhancement} enhances degraded music without
separating it, using general-purpose \acrshort{RIR}s. As noted in
Chapter~\ref{ch02_sota}, no public dataset captures the acoustics of concert
venues, nor the noise of a crowd --- the two gaps this chapter sets out to fill.
Related efforts underline the value of doing so: the Cadenza challenges
\citep{roa2025first} have shown that \acrshort{MSS} can assist \acrshortpl{HA} users,
a use case that extends naturally to the live setting.

\section{CrowdioSet: an audience noise dataset}
\label{sec:crowdioset}

The first ingredient of a live recording that a studio dataset lacks is the
\emph{audience}. Through informal listening to concert recordings made from the
crowd, we identified three categories of audience sound: \emph{ambiences} --- the
room tone and background chatter that are almost always present; \emph{events}
--- punctual reactions such as applause, cheering and shouts of approval or
disapproval; and \emph{sing-alongs} --- passages where the crowd sings the lyrics
together with the performer. CrowdioSet accordingly has two parts: a curated
selection of real Freesound recordings covering \emph{ambiences} and
\emph{events}, which is the core of our contribution and directly addresses
\textbf{RO6}, and a preliminary generative pipeline for \emph{sing-alongs}
(\textbf{RO5}), released as a first step towards the hardest of the three
categories (Section~\ref{sec:mss_limitations}).

\subsection{Ambiences and events}
\label{ssec:mss_ambiences}

We retrieved $11{,}375$ files from Freesound \citep{anastasopoulou2025freesound}
with the queries \emph{crowd}, \emph{audience}, \emph{cheering}, \emph{applause},
\emph{chatter} and \emph{protest}, restricted to clips shorter than eight minutes
and to permissive licences (CC0, CC-BY 3.0/4.0, CC-BY-NC 3.0/4.0 and
Sampling+~1.0), resampling each to $44.1\,\text{kHz}$ $16$-bit stereo. After
manually discarding recordings that could not plausibly occur at a concert
(traffic, nature or machinery), $4{,}819$ files remained, which we labelled as
either \emph{ambiences} or \emph{events} as detailed in
Table~\ref{tab:crowdioset_queries}, amounting to $105$ hours of audience noise.

\subsection{Synthetic sing-alongs}
\label{ssec:mss_singalongs}

Sing-alongs cannot be curated in the same way: isolated recordings of a crowd
singing a known song, cleanly separated from the performance, essentially do not
exist. We therefore \emph{synthesise} them from the \emph{vocals} stems already
present in the source datasets, turning a single lead vocal into a plausible
crowd singing along, through the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:mss_pipeline}. For
each of the $N = 384$ \emph{vocals} stems in MUSDB18-HQ and MOISESDB we combine
two techniques. The first is the Antares AVOX Choir plugin, a choir effect
(vibrato, detuning and delay) in its $32$-voice configuration. The second, and
the core of \textbf{RO5}, uses a pool of $I = 200$ vocal samples retrieved from
Freesound (queries \emph{a cappella} and \emph{vocals}) to convert each vocals
stem with the pre-trained zero-shot voice-conversion model HQ-SVC
\citep{bai2026hq}: half of the conversions preserve the original $f_0$ contour
(in tune, but timbrally similar to the lead) and half adapt it to the range of
the zero-shot sample (timbrally diverse, but less in tune).

A realistic crowd should be \emph{timbrally diverse} yet only \emph{loosely
coupled} to the lead melody, so we rank the conversions by a score balancing
these two criteria. For each candidate $v_i$ and the original stem $v_o$ we
compute a chroma mean absolute error $d_i = \mathrm{MAE}(\Phi(v_i), \Phi(v_o))$,
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the $12$-bin chromagram (a $2048$-sample Hann window,
hop~$512$), and a singer-identity similarity
$s_i = \cos(\Psi(v_i), \Psi(v_o))$, where $\Psi(\cdot)$ is the singer embedding
of \citet{torres2023singer}. A small $d_i$ means the candidate agrees with the
lead on pitch content, while a small $s_i$ means a \emph{distinct} vocal
identity; standardising both across the $N \cdot I$ pool and sorting by
$\arg\min(\tilde{d}_i + \tilde{s}_i)$ favours voices that are in tune yet
timbrally different from the lead.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{ch05_mss/figures/scnet_live.pdf}
  \caption{Data generation pipeline for the \emph{audience} source. The offline
  stage (green) synthesises a \emph{sing-along} for every \emph{vocals} stem, by
  combining zero-shot singing-voice conversions from the HQ-SVC model with the
  $32$-voice AVOX Choir effect, and is run once before training. The online stage
  (blue), executed inside the data loader at every iteration, assembles the
  \emph{audience} stem by drawing and gain-scaling the pre-computed

  of CrowdioSet, so that each training example sees a fresh audience.}
  \label{fig:mss_pipeline}
\end{figure}

The best $64$ candidates are then humanised independently to break their
unnatural synchrony. Using onset detection \citep{mcfee2025librosa} on $v_o$ to
locate syllables, we perturb each syllable's gain by $\mathcal{U}(0.3, 1)$ and
its timing by delays of $\mathcal{U}(0, 0.5)\,\text{s}$, delay each whole voice
by a further $\mathcal{U}(0, 0.3)\,\text{s}$, and pan it with independent
left/right gains $\mathcal{U}(0, 1)$ before summing all voices into a stereo mix.
This HQ-SVC mix is finally balanced by ear with the AVOX Choir mix to form the
\emph{sing-along}.

Sing-alongs are provided for every MOISESDB song and for the MUSDB18-HQ training
set. At training time the \emph{audience} stem is assembled \emph{on the fly}
inside the data loader: starting from silence, each of the three components
(sing-along, ambience, event) is added independently with probability
$p_v = p_a = p_e = 0.5$ and a random gain $\mathcal{U}(0, 1)$. This stochastic
assembly exposes the model to an effectively unlimited variety of audience
conditions without storing pre-mixed material, while for the MUSDB18-HQ test and
validation sets we instead provide fixed, pre-generated audience mixes to keep
evaluation reproducible\footnote{\url{https://doi.org/10.57967/hf/9773}}.

\section[PaRIRset RIRs]{PaRIRset: a concert-venue impulse response dataset}
\label{sec:parirset}

The other component absent from studio data is the venue acoustics. 
Following \citet{kandpal2022musicenhancement}, one could reuse the \acrshort{RIR}
collections common in \acrfull{SE} --- the ACE Challenge \citep{eaton2015ace}, the
MIT IR Survey \citep{traer2016statistics} and the OpenSLR28 corpus
\citep{ko2017study}, the same real \acrshortpl{RIR} we evaluated on in
Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs} --- but these are ill-suited to live music in two ways. First,
the rooms they capture (offices, classrooms, lecture halls) bear little
resemblance to concert venues. Second, and more fundamentally, modern live sound is delivered through a \acrfull{PA} system, typically formed by left and right main hangs of a line array, complemented by flown or ground-stacked subwoofers and point-source
frontfill loudspeakers, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:mss_pa}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth,trim={4cm 0cm 2cm 4cm},clip]{ch05_mss/figures/pa_system_1.pdf}
  \caption{Schematic of a typical concert \acrfull{PA} system. Two \emph{line
  arrays}, flown left and right, cover the
  audience where the \emph{front of house} position normally is; ground-stacked \emph{subwoofers} reinforce the low end; and 
  \emph{frontfills} on the stage lip cover the first rows.}
  \label{fig:mss_pa}
\end{figure}

Because no \acrshort{SE} dataset contains real \acrshortpl{RIR} from concert venues, we therefore measured our own set of responses, the \emph{Public Address Room Impulse Response Set}
(PaRIRset) --- to our knowledge, the first dataset of real \acrshortpl{RIR}
captured in professional concert venues through their \acrshort{PA} systems, and
the contribution behind \textbf{RO7}.

PaRIRset covers $40$ professional concert venues across a wide geographical area
(Figure~\ref{fig:mss_map}) and, more importantly, a wide range of \acrshort{PA}
systems. Table~\ref{tab:parirset_meta} summarises the collection: it spans $16$
manufacturers (d\&b~audiotechnik, L-Acoustics, Nexo, TW~Audio and
Electro-Voice, among others) and $35$ distinct main-array models, from compact
two-element-per-side systems in small clubs to $24$-element arena arrays, with
$0$ to $12$ subwoofers per venue and frontfills present in $24$ of them; $9$ of
the $40$ venues were measured outdoors. The full per-venue detail --- location,
indoor or outdoor, and the specific model and element count of every main, sub
and frontfill --- is listed in Appendix~\ref{app:parirset}
(Tables~\ref{tab:parirset_full_a} and~\ref{tab:parirset_full_b}). We release the
raw mono and multichannel recordings at $48\,\text{kHz}$\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/enricguso/parirset_dev}}, together with the
processed, augmented stereo \acrshortpl{RIR} used for training, downsampled to
$44.1\,\text{kHz}$ to match the source-separation corpora\footnote{\url{https://doi.org/10.57967/hf/9774}}.

\subsection{Measurement methodology}
\label{ssec:mss_measurement}

Access to professional venues and their \acrshort{PA} systems is tightly
constrained --- typically a short window during a soundcheck --- so we limited each
measurement to a single exponential sine sweep per \acrshort{PA} side (left and
right). The microphone was placed at the \acrfull{FOH} position, next to the
mixing console, which is the reference listening spot in the room. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:mss_setup}, we captured each sweep with a Zylia ZM-1 microphone
array alongside a beyerdynamic MM1 measurement microphone through a Zoom AMS-24
interface. 



The \acrshortpl{RIR} were obtained with the exponential-sine-sweep method
\citep{farina2000simultaneous} (Section~\ref{sec:bg_rir_sweep}), with $f_1 =
20\,\text{Hz}$, $f_2 = 20\,\text{kHz}$ and a sweep duration of $6\,\text{s}$.
After deconvolution, two post-processing steps were applied. First, we reduced
noise by windowing each \acrshort{RIR} where its envelope fell below the noise
floor, keeping $100\,\text{ms}$ before the direct sound and applying
$50\,\text{ms}$ fades at the start and before the noise-floor onset. Second,
because the \acrshort{FOH} position is not always equidistant from the two
\acrshort{PA} sides, the left and right \acrshortpl{RIR} were time-aligned by
cross-correlation. Finally, taking the flat-response beyerdynamic MM1 as a
reference, we corrected the colouration of the Zylia-derived responses with a
second-order high-shelf filter ($f_0 = 3.5\,\text{kHz}$, gain $-4\,\text{dB}$,
$Q = 0.5$).

A subtlety of this per-side procedure is worth clarifying. In a typical
\acrshort{PA}, only the main hangs are strictly left and right; the subwoofers
and frontfills are usually fed from a matrix of the two channels (most often a
mono $\text{L}+\text{R}$ sum). Measuring the left side therefore does not
isolate the left main hang alone: the captured response also contains the
contribution of any system driven by that channel through the matrix. At the
\acrshort{FOH} position this is acceptable, and in fact desirable. The
frontfills, aimed at the first rows and far from \acrshort{FOH}, contribute
negligibly there, so they do not meaningfully colour the measurement; the
subwoofers, on the other hand, do reach \acrshort{FOH} and their low-frequency
energy is genuinely part of what a listener --- and an audience recording ---
receives at that position. Capturing the mains together with the subs, as our
sweeps do, thus yields a stereo pair of responses that reflects the full
low-to-high-frequency field actually present at \acrshort{FOH}, which is exactly
what we want to convolve our sources with.

\subsection{RIR Augmentation}
\label{ssec:mss_augmentation}

Forty measurements are too few to train on directly, so we explored four
augmentation strategies of increasing scope. $D_1$ operates on the beyerdynamic
\acrshortpl{RIR} alone: splitting each response $h$ at
$\arg\max(|h|) + 2\,\text{ms}$ into a direct part $d_h$ and a tail $t_h$, we
generate variants by flipping polarity and rescaling the tail by gains
$g = \{-6, -1.5, 0, 1.5, 3.5\}\,\text{dB}$, augmenting each measurement $20$-fold.
$D_2$ adds the raw signals of the $19$ Zylia capsules ($\text{zylia}_{\text{caps}}$),
each with a slightly different position; $D_3$ adds $19$ virtual microphones
synthesised by the array's proprietary beamformer with random orientations and
polar patterns ($\text{zylia}_{\text{beams}}$); and $D_4$ permutes the direct
part $d_h$ of one venue with the tail $t_h$ of another (in what we call $\text{PA}_{\text{permute}}$).

To assess them, we retrained an SCNet \citep{tong2024scnet} model from scratch on
MUSDB18-HQ with default hyperparameters, convolving all sources and targets with
the \acrshortpl{RIR} and adding one augmentation set at a time; we evaluated on
$8$ held-out venues (augmented with $D_1$) forming the PaRIRset test set, and
report the \acrfull{SDR} across all sources. The results in Figure~\ref{fig:mss_ablation} 
yield three observations.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim={2.4cm 0.8cm 0.2cm 0.2cm}, clip, width=0.9\columnwidth]{ch05_mss/figures/parirset_sdr.pdf}
  \caption{Ablation of the PaRIRset augmentation strategies, adding one set at a
  time. Significance from paired $t$-tests: \textasteriskcentered\ $p \leq 0.05$,
  \textasteriskcentered\textasteriskcentered\ $p \leq 0.01$; error bars are
  $95\%$ confidence intervals.}
  \label{fig:mss_ablation}
\end{figure}

First,
every configuration that includes \acrshortpl{RIR} significantly outperforms the
no-reverberation baseline, confirming that exposing the model to acoustics is what matters most. 
Second, the four strategies differ little from one another, which suggests that \emph{including} real venue
responses matters more than the particular way they are augmented. Third, 
$D_3$ (the beamformed virtual microphones) improves significantly over the
already strong \acrshort{SE}-only baseline (paired $t$-test, $p = 0.027$) 
supporting the premise behind \textbf{RO7}. A
plausible reason for this is that its randomly oriented polar patterns inject directional
diversity that the raw capsule signals of $D_2$ do not, though our evidence does
not let us claim this conclusively. Conversely, $D_4$ ($\text{PA}_{\text{permute}}$)
brought no gain and was discarded; recombining the direct sound of one venue with
the tail of another appears not to create useful new variety.
Henceforth, \emph{PaRIRset} denotes the combination of the \acrshort{SE}
\acrshortpl{RIR}, $D_1$, $D_2$ and $D_3$, totalling approximately $2{,}200$ stereo
\acrshortpl{RIR}.


A final subtlety concerns the reverberation level. Convolving every source with a
full-length response produced mixtures that were audibly too reverberant, because
PaRIRset was measured in \emph{empty} venues during soundcheck, whereas a full
audience shortens reverberation substantially \citep{beranek1960audience}. To
approximate the occupied condition, we attenuate the tail of each \acrshort{RIR}
--- that is, we scale every sample except the direct-sound peak at
$\arg\max(|h|)$, leaving the direct sound untouched while reducing the
reflections --- by a random $\mathcal{U}(0, 1)$ factor during training and by a
fixed $-6\,\text{dB}$ for evaluation, which we found to produce a more realistic
reverberation level by ear.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:mss_setup}

Our clean sources come from two studio corpora. MUSDB18-HQ
\citep{rafii2019musdbhq} is the standard \acrshort{MSS} benchmark: $150$
full-length stereo tracks at $44.1\,\text{kHz}$ ($100$ for training, $50$ for
testing), each provided as four isolated stems --- \emph{vocals}, \emph{drums},
\emph{bass} and \emph{other}. From the $100$ training tracks we hold out the
conventional $14$-track validation split.\footnote{Actions~--~One Minute Smile;
Alexander Ross~--~Goodbye Bolero; ANiMAL~--~Rockshow; Clara Berry And
Wooldog~--~Waltz For My Victims; Fergessen~--~Nos Palpitants; James May~--~On
The Line; Johnny Lokke~--~Promises \& Lies; Leaf~--~Summerghost; Meaxic~--~Take
A Step; Patrick Talbot~--~A Reason To Leave; Skelpolu~--~Human Mistakes; Traffic
Experiment~--~Sirens; Triviul~--~Angelsaint; Young Griffo~--~Pennies.}
MOISESDB \citep{pereira2023moisesdb} is a larger and more recent corpus of
$240$ tracks, but with a finer, hierarchical stem taxonomy (vocals, bass, drums,
guitar, piano, and further categories). To co-train it with MUSDB18-HQ we
\emph{parse} it into the same four-stem taxonomy, mapping vocals, drums and bass
directly and collapsing all remaining instruments into \emph{other}. Together
with CrowdioSet for audience noise and PaRIRset for venue acoustics, these
constitute the training material for a first \emph{Live} \acrshort{MSS}
baseline. As in the PaRIRset ablation, we adopt SCNet \citep{tong2024scnet} as
the backbone (Section~\ref{sec:sota_mss}): it is open-source and among the most
competitive models on MUSDB18-HQ, and thus a realistic starting point.

\subsection{Training details}
\label{ssec:mss_training}

All models are trained from scratch with SCNet's default hyperparameters, save
for a few adjustments. When co-training on both MUSDB18-HQ and MOISESDB we lower the
initial learning rate from $5\times10^{-4}$ to $4\times10^{-4}$, as prescribed
by \citet{tong2024scnet}, and we remove the exponential moving average of the
weights, which simplifies the pipeline at negligible cost: our co-trained
baseline reaches $9.0\,\text{dB}$ \acrshort{SDR} on the MUSDB18-HQ test set,
matching the $9.0\,\text{dB}$ reported for SCNet trained on MUSDB18-HQ alone and
close to the $9.2\,\text{dB}$ of its co-training setting. Each model is trained
for $200$ epochs on a single NVIDIA L40S \acrshort{GPU} (about seven days per
model), with SCNet's default random time-shift augmentation of up to two
seconds.

To expose the model to as many source combinations as possible, we use batch
sizes of $10$ or $8$ (with or without audience), split into two groups per batch
so that sources can be remixed across songs within a batch \citep{jeon2024does}.
This cross-song remixing is standard practice in \acrshort{MSS}, but its benefit
is not universal: \citet{jeon2024does} show that it stems from the increased
diversity of otherwise \emph{inconsistent} mixtures, and that for highly
consistent sources it can help far less --- in vocal-harmony separation, where
sources are especially correlated, they note that random mixing was even found
``detrimental on a small dataset''. This is a concern in our setting,
because our synthetic \emph{sing-alongs} are, by construction, strongly
correlated with one another and with the lead vocal (some converted voices sing
in near-unison, differing only in timbre), so remixing the \emph{audience}
source across songs might not help, or might even hurt it. We therefore trained
the model both with and without remixing and compared them
(Table~\ref{tab:mss_remix}). Remixing left the \emph{audience} source
essentially unchanged ($+0.07\,\text{dB}$ \acrshort{SDR}, paired $t$-test
$p = 0.07$) while improving the musical sources overall, so we retained it for all subsequent experiments.

The core of the experiment is a comparison of four training configurations,
which differ only in the degradations applied to the same clean sources and
which we name after them:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{clean}: MUSDB18-HQ + MOISESDB;
    \item \textbf{rev}: (MUSDB18-HQ + MOISESDB) $\circledast$ PaRIRset;
    \item \textbf{noisy}: MUSDB18-HQ + MOISESDB + CrowdioSet;
    \item \textbf{noisyrev}: (MUSDB18-HQ + MOISESDB + CrowdioSet) $\circledast$ PaRIRset;
\end{itemize}
where $\circledast$ denotes convolution with a PaRIRset \acrshort{RIR}. Both the
audience mixing and the reverberation are applied \emph{inside} the training
loop, as described in Sections~\ref{sec:crowdioset} and~\ref{sec:parirset}, so
that every epoch presents novel combinations of sources, audiences and rooms.
Because the added degradations make optimisation progressively harder, we lower
the initial learning rate accordingly across conditions, to $\{4, 3.5, 2, 2\}
\times 10^{-4}$ for \textbf{clean}, \textbf{rev}, \textbf{noisy} and
\textbf{noisyrev}, whose best validation checkpoints are reached at epochs
$198$, $199$, $197$ and $144$. In the reverberant conditions our goal is to
\emph{separate} the sources while \emph{preserving} reverberation: both the
targets and the estimates remain reverberant, so the model is not asked to
dereverberate.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{ssec:mss_evaluation}

We evaluate on the MUSDB18-HQ test set --- keeping our results comparable with
prior work --- augmented with the held-out \emph{audience} test stems and the
PaRIRset test set. Reverberation is applied here \emph{deterministically}, so
that every model sees the same conditions: $25\%$ of the samples with no
reverberation, $25\%$ with the full \acrshort{RIR}, and the remaining $50\%$
split equally between tails attenuated by $-6$ and $-12\,\text{dB}$. Each of the
four models is evaluated under the same four conditions (\textbf{clean},
\textbf{rev}, \textbf{noisy}, \textbf{noisyrev}), giving a full cross of training
and test degradations.

To place the audience-separation results in context, we add SAM Audio
\citep{shi2025samaudio} as a baseline capable of isolating arbitrary sources
from a text prompt. We use its large pre-trained variant in text-prompting mode,
with a single re-ranking candidate and non-overlapping $20\,\text{s}$ windows to
fit memory, querying \emph{vocals}, \emph{drums}, \emph{bass}, \emph{other} and
\emph{crowd} (the last outperforming \emph{audience}).

As in SCNet, our primary objective metric is the \acrfull{SDR} \citep{le2019sdr},
computed against non-reverberant or reverberant references according to the
evaluation condition. However, \acrshort{SDR} does not always track perceived
quality \citep{guso2022loss, jaffe2025musical} --- and it is especially
unforgiving of diffusion-based models such as SAM Audio, whose outputs are not
sample-aligned and are therefore penalised regardless of how they sound --- so we
complement it with a subjective AB listening test. To retain statistical power
with a modest sample, we restrict the test to two tasks, \emph{vocals isolation}
and \emph{audience isolation}, on $10$ real audience recordings sourced from
social media and split into two groups of five. For vocals isolation,
participants compared the vocals from our \textbf{noisy} model against those of
the \textbf{clean} baseline; for audience isolation, the audience stem from the
\textbf{noisy} model against SAM Audio; in both cases they chose which
separation they preferred, if any. Twenty-six participants took part --- media
researchers and professional musicians or sound engineers --- using the web-based
AB interface of \citet{zhang_barry_sun_hines_2021}, with the presentation order
of the two systems randomised.

\subsection{Subjective results}
\label{ssec:mss_res_subj}

Figure~\ref{fig:mss_subjective} summarises the AB listening test. Counting ties
as votes for the baseline and applying a one-sided binomial test ($H: P > 0.5$),
listeners significantly preferred our \textbf{noisy} model in both tasks: for
\emph{vocals} isolation, $105$ of $130$ votes ($p < 10^{-12}$); for
\emph{audience} isolation, $119$ of $130$ votes ($p < 10^{-23}$). The subjective
preference is thus far more decisive than the objective margins, consistent with
the known limitations of \acrshort{SDR} on this kind of material, and confirms
that the improvements are perceptually real.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:mss_limitations}

Several limitations temper these results. We did not systematically search the
online mixing parameters (the probabilities $p_v$, $p_a$, $p_e$ and the gains):
the values used were chosen by informal listening and may be suboptimal. More
fundamentally, listening to the model on real concert recordings shows that,
while it separates \emph{ambiences} and \emph{events} effectively --- acting as a
denoiser --- it rarely isolates the \emph{sing-along} reliably. We attribute this
to the synthesis itself: the voice-conversion sing-alongs remain too similar to
the original \emph{vocals} stem (some converted voices sing in exact unison,
differing only in timbre), and the audio effects already present on those stems
(reverb, delay) further blur the boundary between the lead vocal and its
synthetic crowd. A future CrowdioSet could start from \emph{dry} vocals
\citep{zang2025music}, use more expressive synthesis, and model the performers'
reactions to the crowd besides the crowd's reaction to the performance. Finally, the early convergence of the \textbf{noisyrev}
model (epoch $144$ against $\sim\!200$ for the others) hints that the combined
noise-and-reverberation setting may need architectural changes or a fuller
hyperparameter search to be exploited fully.


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:mss_conclusions}

This chapter introduced the \emph{Live} \acrshort{MSS} task --- the separation of music
recorded from within a live audience --- and, in line with the rest of the thesis, tackled
it as a problem of \emph{ecological validity}: studio-trained separators fail on
audience recordings because their training data omits the venue acoustics, the
\acrshort{PA} colouration and the crowd noise that such recordings inevitably
contain. Rather than only collecting scarce and unlabelled real live material, we
closed this gap by \emph{synthesising} those conditions on top of existing
studio datasets.

Concretely, we addressed the three research objectives set out for this chapter:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RO5} --- generating synthetic audiences that sing along --- was
    preliminarly addressed through the voice-conversion pipeline of CrowdioSet, which turns
    the vocals of existing datasets into humanised, timbrally diverse crowd
    sing-alongs;
    \item \textbf{RO6} --- curating audience noise from freely available
    recordings --- was addressed by assembling the ambiences and events of
    CrowdioSet from Freesound, with documented licences and reproducible splits;
    and
    \item \textbf{RO7} --- capturing impulse responses in real concert venues ---
    was addressed with PaRIRset, the first set of stereo \acrshortpl{RIR}
    measured across $40$ concert venues through their \acrshort{PA} systems.
\end{itemize}

Experiments with SCNet show that training with CrowdioSet improves vocal and
audience separation on live recordings while barely affecting clean studio
performance, and that PaRIRset yields statistically significant gains over the
general-purpose \acrshortpl{RIR} used in \acrshort{SE}
(Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}). A listening test confirms the objective findings. As with the previous
chapters, the improvement comes not from a new architecture but from training
data that better reflects the conditions of real use. We release the datasets\footnote{\url{https://doi.org/10.57967/hf/9773}}\footnote{\url{https://doi.org/10.57967/hf/9774}}, the
code and model weights\footnote{\url{https://github.com/enricguso/guso_ismir26}} and
audio examples publicly.\footnote{\url{https://enricguso.github.io/crowdioset_parirset}}
