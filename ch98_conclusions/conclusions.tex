\chapter[General Conclusions and Future Perspectives]{Conclusion and\\future perspectives}
\chaptermark{Conclusions}
\label{ch98_conclusions}

\vspace{3cm}

This dissertation has pursued a single idea across three application fields:
that the \emph{ecological validity} of the data used to train and evaluate \acrshortpl{DNN} (i.e. how faithfully it reflects the conditions of real use) is
as decisive as the models themselves, and sometimes a more accessible lever for
improving them. Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing} applied this idea to the
\emph{evaluation} of \acrshortpl{HA}, Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs} to the
\emph{training} data of monaural \acrshort{SE}, and
Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss} to both evaluation and training, in the newly framed task of Live Music Source
Separation. 

This chapter summarizes the contributions across the three research fields and the
seven Research Objectives (RO) they addressed
(Section~\ref{sec:outline}). Because in the \acrshort{MIR} field a lot can change during the course of four years, we then update the changes in the \acrshort{SOTA} for each published work to discuss how the field has moved since:
which of our intuitions proved well-grounded, where industry has converged on the same
questions, and which limitations have become apparent only in hindsight
(Section~\ref{sec:conc_recent}). Widening the scope further, we reflect on the
broader relevance of ecological validity beyond the three tasks studied here (Section~\ref{sec:conc_relevance}) and, finally, we outline the directions we find most promising going forward, towards
deployable, interactive tools built on ecologically valid synthetic data
(Section~\ref{sec:conc_future}), and close with some final thoughts.

\section{Summary of contributions}
\label{sec:conc_contributions}
This thesis set out to improve the \emph{ecological validity} of the synthetic
datasets used to train and evaluate \acrshortpl{DNN} for audio processing,
across three application fields. A common constraint shaped how we pursued it:
as a small, compute-constrained academic group, we could not compete on model
scale or compute, so we competed on \emph{data} instead --- building material
that better reflects real use --- and released every dataset, decoder, model and
recording, so that the resulting improvements are available to any laboratory.
All seven research objectives were addressed; as the summaries below make clear,
this does not mean all were answered affirmatively --- a negative or inconclusive
result resolves an objective as much as a positive one.

\paragraph{\acrfullpl{HA} (Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing}).}
Our main contribution here is a \emph{methodology} for ecologically valid
evaluation of \acrshort{HA} \acrfull{SE}: we reproduce complex speech-in-noise
scenes over a loudspeaker array using Ambisonics and \acrshort{VBAP}, capture
them on real devices, and compare the devices' own processing against
\acrshort{DNN}-based enhancement under conditions closer to real use than the
stationary laboratory tests devices are usually certified with.
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{RO1}] \emph{Expose the performance gap between conventional
    \acrshort{HA} enhancement and \acrshort{DNN}-based approaches in
    ecologically valid, complex acoustic scenes.} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Addressed}
    $\rightarrow$ the evaluation revealed a clear gap, with conventional
    enhancement occasionally performing worse than no processing at all.
    \item[\textbf{RO2}] \emph{Investigate the potential of \acrshort{DNN}-based
    \acrshort{SE} to outperform conventional \acrshort{HA} processing.}
    $\rightarrow$ \textit{Addressed affirmatively} $\rightarrow$ \acrshortpl{DNN}
    substantially improved denoising and objective intelligibility, and the
    causal variant retained most of that advantage.
\end{itemize}
Beyond these findings, the lasting contribution is the evaluation methodology
itself, and the case it makes for pursuing \acrshort{DNN}-based \acrshort{HA}
enhancement: to support further work in this direction we released a
\acrshort{HA} \acrshort{HRTF} set, 10th-order Ambisonics-to-binaural
\acrshort{BiMagLS} decoders, a synthetic binaural training corpus and a
reverberant test set with the recordings of each device.

\paragraph{Monaural \acrfull{SE} (Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}).}
Within the GuestXR project, we asked which properties of a synthetic
\acrshort{RIR} drive generalisation to real rooms, training one model per
dataset on six controlled \acrshort{RIR} sets.
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{RO3}] \emph{Determine whether frequency-dependent absorption
    and source or receiver directivity improve generalisation to real
    acoustics.} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Addressed with a mixed result}
    $\rightarrow$ frequency-dependent absorption brings the clearest benefit,
    whereas the evidence on source and receiver directivity was inconclusive.
    \item[\textbf{RO4}] \emph{Determine whether fewer but geometrically more
    realistic mesh-based \acrshortpl{RIR} are preferable to abundant \acrfull{ISM}
    ones.} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Addressed with a negative result} $\rightarrow$
    mesh-based realism did not compensate for a smaller variety and quantity of
    rooms, though a mesh-based set that is also varied would be needed to fully
    disentangle the two (Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}).
\end{itemize}
We released the resulting MB-RIRs dataset, among the most downloaded resources
of the GuestXR project repository.

\paragraph{Live \acrfull{MSS} (Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss}).}
More in line with the research of the \acrshort{MTG}, we formulated \emph{Live}
\acrshort{MSS} (i.e.\ separating music recorded from within a live audience) and
made it trainable by synthesising live conditions on top of studio data.
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{RO5}] \emph{Generate synthetic audiences that sing along,
    through singing-voice conversion.} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Addressed as a first
    step} $\rightarrow$ the voice-conversion pipeline produces usable
    sing-alongs, though reliably isolating them remains difficult and is left as
    a limitation.
    \item[\textbf{RO6}] \emph{Curate audience noise from freely available
    recordings.} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Addressed affirmatively} $\rightarrow$ the
    curated ambiences and events of CrowdioSet improve separation on live
    recordings while barely affecting clean studio performance.
    \item[\textbf{RO7}] \emph{Determine whether impulse responses measured in
    real concert venues improve over general-purpose ones.} $\rightarrow$
    \textit{Addressed affirmatively} $\rightarrow$ PaRIRset yielded statistically
    significant gains over the \acrshort{SE} \acrshortpl{RIR} of
    Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}.
\end{itemize}

For convenience, all the datasets, models, decoders and recordings released
throughout this thesis are collected, with their repositories and licences, in
Appendix~\ref{app:materials}.

\section{Recent developments and closing discussion}
\label{sec:conc_recent}
We now revisit each of the three fields we have addressed, discussing how its \acrshort{SOTA} has evolved since our work was published.

\subsection{Hearing aids}
\label{ssec:conc_ha}

Since the evaluation of Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing} was published, the Clarity
Challenge series has established itself as the main forum for
\acrshort{DNN}-based \acrshort{HA} enhancement, and its findings echo ours.
Evaluated, like our study, with \acrshort{HASPI} and \acrshort{HASQI}
\citep{cox2023clarity}, its editions reached the same two conclusions we drew:
conventional \acrshort{HA} processing struggles in complex, noisy scenes, and
\acrshort{DNN}-based enhancement has the potential to improve on it. Two details
are worth noting. First, \acrshort{HASQI} was added alongside \acrshort{HASPI}
precisely because some systems raised intelligibility at the expense of
naturalness \citep{cox2023clarity} --- the same tension our follow-up test
surfaced between \emph{strong} and \emph{mild} enhancement
(Section~\ref{sec:ha_followup}). Second, and most relevant to this thesis, that
challenge paired a simulated evaluation set with one based on real-room
measurements, and found that systems improving on the simulated data degraded
sharply on the measured recordings \citep{cox2023clarity} --- an independent,
large-scale instance of the ecological-validity gap that motivated our
loudspeaker-based evaluation.

Our own contribution --- a dataset centred on binaural \acrshortpl{RIR} for
\acrshortpl{HA} \acrshort{SE} --- was mirrored, the same year, by BinauRec
\citep{delebecque2023binaurec}, which likewise studies the influence of
\acrshortpl{RIR} on binaural speech enhancement, and, the following year, by an
extension to the multichannel case \citep{westhausen2024binaural}. That the same
idea was pursued independently and elsewhere suggests the topic was a timely one.

A second, clear trend is the tightening focus on the real-time and low-latency
constraints of hearing devices, which we identified as the main obstacle to
deployment. Multi-stage systems now meet the sub-$5\,\text{ms}$ latency budget
while improving \acrshort{HASPI} and \acrshort{HASQI} over the challenge baseline
\citep{multistage2023lowlatency}, and this push has continued towards
\emph{sub-millisecond} latency on hearables \citep{dementyev2024submillisecond}.
In the same spirit as our own remark that model size remains a bottleneck for
hearing aids, some works have gone as far as transmitting only a low-bit-rate
binaural link between the two devices to afford the enhancement
\citep{westhausen2023lowbitrate}.

Overall, the questions our study raised --- how to evaluate \acrshort{HA}
enhancement under ecologically valid conditions, and how to make
\acrshort{DNN}-based processing deployable on-device --- remain central to the
field. Our work has since been taken up in directions ranging from optimised
filter design for low-power devices \citep{rawandale2024improving} to
broader surveys of the area \citep{jespersen2023hearing, kanev2024speech, cao2025aecrn}, which
suggests our study has helped inform the work that followed.

\subsection{Speech enhancement}
\label{ssec:conc_se}

The premise of Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs} --- that the realism of synthetic
\acrshortpl{RIR}, and frequency-dependent absorption in particular, improves
generalisation to real rooms --- has since been taken up, and largely confirmed,
by industry. The mesh-based responses we used come from SoundSpaces
\citep{chen2022soundspaces}, a simulator developed at Meta; and Treble
Technologies has released a commercial wave-based simulation \acrshort{SDK}
together with large, physically accurate \acrshort{RIR} datasets built on exactly
the same rationale \citep{mullins2025treble10}. Most tellingly, a Treble study
reproduced our experiment at a larger scale and in the multichannel far-field
setting: training the same enhancement model on \acrshortpl{RIR} of increasing
simulation fidelity, they report up to a $38\%$ relative reduction in word error
rate on \emph{measured} data for the high-fidelity set
\citep{gotz2026improving}. Both works cite \acrshort{MB-RIRs}, placing it in
the lineage of increasing \acrshort{RIR} realism alongside GWA
\citep{tang2022gwa} and other efforts.

Seen in this light, our contribution was less a wholly novel simulator than an
ecologically valid \acrshort{RIR} set made openly available, so that
compute-constrained groups could benefit from the same idea that industry was
pursuing behind commercial \acrshortpl{SDK}. Made freely downloadable,
\acrshort{MB-RIRs} has since become the most downloaded resource in the GuestXR
project's Zenodo repository, which we take as a sign that open data of this kind
meets a real need. The comparison with Treble is also instructive about our own
limitations: their focus on far-field, multichannel conditions is not incidental
--- it is there, rather than in our near-field monaural setup, that the gap
between simulation methods is widest, which may be why the source- and
receiver-directivity benefits we looked for turned out inconclusive
(\textbf{RO3}). The effect we sought was likely clearest precisely where we did
not look.

A related point concerns \textbf{RO4}. We found that abundant \acrshort{ISM}
rooms outperformed fewer mesh-based ones, but the mesh-based set available to us
(SoundSpaces) was also the least varied, confounding realism with diversity. The
natural way to disentangle the two is a mesh-based set that is itself large and
varied, such as GWA \citep{tang2022gwa}, which we did not evaluate, as at the
time it sat outside our usual audio venues, having been presented in the
computer-graphics community. Rather than a mere oversight, the growing interest
in GWA reinforces our reading that such datasets are valuable: the Treble
challenge mixes Treble and GWA rooms \citep{lin2025generative}, and the
multichannel study lists GWA beside \acrshort{MB-RIRs} \citep{gotz2026improving}.
We see the most promising direction not as choosing between paradigms but as
\emph{combining} them --- for instance, the roughly $20{,}000$ mesh-based rooms of
GWA together with the $60{,}000$ \acrshort{ISM} rooms of \acrshort{MB-RIRs} ---
pairing geometric realism with sheer variety, and we encourage the community to
explore it.

More broadly, room-acoustic data has become a remarkably active research area
since our work. On the simulation and measurement side, new resources keep
appearing: Meta released a $1{,}000$-room corpus for scene-wide acoustic
parameter estimation \citep{falconperez2025scenewide}, open ray-tracing toolkits
such as GSound-SIR \citep{zang2025gsound} lower the barrier to spatial
\acrshort{RIR} synthesis, and measured databases now capture more challenging
conditions, including moving microphones along a controlled trajectory
\citep{damiano2025trajectorir}. In parallel, a complementary line attacks the
problem from the estimation side, inferring \acrshortpl{RIR} or their parameters
blindly from reverberant speech \citep{gotz2026matching, wang2026recrir}.
Together with the industrial efforts above, this activity confirms that the
realism and coverage of room-acoustic data --- the very concern of
Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs} --- is now widely recognised as a problem worth
addressing.

\subsection{Music source separation}
\label{ssec:conc_mss}

Being the most recent of the three studies, our work on live \acrshort{MSS}
(Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss}) has the least state of the art to catch up with. What
has been research during this thesis is largely orthogonal to our contribution, which concerns
\emph{data} rather than architecture. On the modelling side, band-split
\acrshort{RoPE} transformers have consolidated their lead on studio benchmarks
\citep{lu2024bsroformer}, and lightweight variants now bring comparable quality
to real-time and embedded settings --- the low-latency line we distinguished from
our own use of \emph{live} in Section~\ref{sec:sota_mss}. Because our proposal is
a training and evaluation recipe rather than a network, these advances are
complementary: any of these separators could be trained on CrowdioSet and
PaRIRset in place of the \acrshort{SCNet} backbone we used.

More closely related in spirit is the emergence of Music Source Restoration
\citep{zang2025music}, run as an \acrshort{ICASSP}~2026 challenge, which likewise
rejects the linear-mixing assumption --- there, to undo studio production
artefacts rather than to add live-venue degradations. The two settings are
complementary halves of the same idea: that the gap between clean stems and the
signals encountered in the wild must be modelled explicitly, whether that gap
comes from a mastering chain or from a concert \acrshort{PA} and its audience.
Beyond this conceptual parallel, their dataset would also be of direct practical
use to us: as noted in Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss}, the \emph{dry} vocal stems it
provides would be an ideal starting point for recreating our \emph{sing-alongs},
which our current pipeline synthesises from already-processed vocals. Finally,
generative approaches to separation have begun to appear, synthesising rather
than masking the target source \citep{plaja2025generating}; as discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:conc_future}, we see this as a promising route to the more
realistic sing-alongs that our own pipeline still struggles to isolate.

\section{The broader relevance of ecological validity}
\label{sec:conc_relevance}

The concern that runs through this thesis --- the importance of ecological
validity --- is not specific to the three tasks we studied. It resurfaces, often
unremarked, wherever a field quietly substitutes convenient data for
representative data. Two examples from music information retrieval make the point.

Automatic music mixing is the first. The field has seen remarkable progress:
recent systems repurpose out-of-domain data to overcome the scarcity of dry/wet
pairs \citep{martinez2022automatic}, model the one-to-many nature of mixing with
generative approaches \citep{moliner2026automatic}, and learn transferable mixing
styles directly from separated stems \citep{cheng2026stemfx}, with listening
tests that increasingly approach human-level quality. These are genuine
breakthroughs. Yet across this work it is rarely made explicit \emph{which}
human, mixing \emph{which} music. In practice, the reference for a
``professional mix'' is very often MUSDB18-HQ \citep{rafii2019musdbhq}: a corpus
assembled for source \emph{separation}, whose mixtures were never selected for
being exemplary. Training or evaluating automatic mixing on it thereby equates
``professional'' with a body of mostly amateur or semi-professional rock and pop
recordings by a handful of engineers --- a bias acknowledged even by the community
that popularised the dataset, which notes its focus on Western pop and its
limited pool of mixing engineers \citep{fabbro2024sound}. A model that matches
such references has learned to reproduce competent demos, not the mixing
decisions behind commercially successful records.

\vspace{0.5cm}

The second example is music identification, where the stakes are directly
economic: audio fingerprinting underpins the monitoring of public performances
for royalty distribution. Classical fingerprints are designed to recognise the
exact studio master and do not generalise to live renditions, where venue
reverberation, a \acrshort{PA} system and crowd noise reshape the signal.
\citet{araz2025enhancing} tackle this head-on, improving neural fingerprint
robustness by training with realistic room acoustics and evaluating on real
microphone recordings made across bars, nightclubs and concert halls --- and, in
doing so, uncovering evaluation flaws in a prior system that had flattered its
reported performance. Yet even this careful work inherits the limitation at the
heart of our Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss}: its room acoustics come from general-purpose
\acrshort{RIR} datasets such as OpenAIR \citep{murphy2010openair}, measured in
halls and churches \emph{without} a live \acrshort{PA} system. Such responses
capture a room's reverberation but not the colouration of the line arrays and
subwoofers through which concert audiences actually hear music, nor the crowd
that accompanies it --- precisely the gap PaRIRset and CrowdioSet were built to
fill. Tellingly, the authors themselves note that at festival distances it is the
low frequencies from the speakers that survive the crowd noise, yet the acoustics
they train on contain no such system.

The parallel with our own domains is exact. Just as training speech enhancement
on oversimplified \acrshortpl{RIR} teaches a model to undo a reverberation that
does not occur in real rooms (Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}), training an automatic
mixer on incidental mixtures teaches it to imitate a target that no listener
would call a great mix, and training a fingerprinter without a \acrshort{PA}
teaches it to recognise a concert it will never actually hear. The failure is not
in the models but in the implicit claim that the available data stands in for the
real objective. It is the cooking-school version of learning to cook by averaging
the recipes of whichever restaurants happen to be nearby: one may become fluent,
and still never learn to cook well.

None of this is a criticism of these methods, several of which are genuinely
sophisticated; it is an encouragement to spend as much care on the ecological
validity of evaluation data as on the models themselves. Stating which
distribution the data is meant to represent, and how far it actually does, would
sharpen these claims, exactly as it sharpened the questions we asked about
hearing aids, reverberation and live music. It is, we believe, the most
transferable recommendation of this thesis.


\section{Future directions and final thoughts}
\label{sec:conc_future}

In light of the discussion above, several next steps follow naturally.

\paragraph{Growing PaRIRset, CrowdioSet and its sing-alongs.}
CrowdioSet has proven surprisingly effective for the modest effort we invested in
it, which makes it the natural place to build. The obvious first step is to
enlarge it and, above all, to improve the synthetic sing-alongs, whose isolation
remains its weakest point (\textbf{RO5}). Starting from clean \emph{dry} vocal
stems, such as those released for Music Source Restoration
\citep{zang2025music}, would remove the processing our current pipeline inherits
from already-mixed vocals. So far we have been unable to generate convincing
sing-alongs with general-purpose generative audio models such as Stable Audio
\citep{evans2024stable}; should these mature, they would offer a route to far
more varied and realistic crowds. Combined with tiny, deployable architectures,
richer versions of these datasets could support on-device denoising for the
hearing impaired, connecting the live-music work of Chapter~\ref{ch05_mss} back
to the hearing aids of Chapter~\ref{ch03_hearing}. Likewise, should PaRIRset gain
traction and find use in other tasks, we intend to keep measuring further venues
and to release a larger second version in the future.

\paragraph{Synthetic pre-training with PaRIRset as real-data reward.}
A recent and, to us, compelling recipe comes from MuScriptor
\citep{rouard2026muscriptor}, a multi-instrument transcription model trained in
three stages: pre-training on abundant \emph{synthetic} data, fine-tuning on
\emph{real} recordings, and a final reinforcement-learning stage that aligns the
model using only a small, manually verified set of high-quality examples. Its
guiding principle --- ``data, not architecture'' --- is exactly ours. We see a
direct application to \acrshort{RIR}-based training: a speech-enhancement or
separation model could be pre-trained on abundant synthetic responses ---
combining the mesh-based rooms of GWA \citep{tang2022gwa} with the \acrshort{ISM}
rooms of \acrshort{MB-RIRs} (Chapter~\ref{ch04_mbrirs}) --- and then refined with
a reinforcement-learning stage rewarded on the smaller set of \emph{real} concert
measurements in PaRIRset. This would let a large synthetic corpus supply
coverage while a handful of real measurements supply the final, ecologically
valid calibration --- turning the synthetic-versus-real trade-off of this thesis
into a pipeline rather than a choice.

\paragraph{A discarded chapter: ecologically valid audio doubling.}
The initial plan for this thesis included a fourth study, on \emph{automatic
audio doubling}, which we ultimately set aside but which this recipe could
revive. When recording an album, musicians commonly play the same part --- a
rhythm guitar, say --- two or more times and pan the takes left and right, to
obtain a wider, larger-than-life sound. The traditional Digital Signal Processing (DSP) methods that imitate
this effect simply add short, flipped-polarity delays; because the copy is identical to
the original, their high correlation produces the hollow colouration of comb
filtering. When a human plays the part twice, the small imperfections between
takes lower that correlation and yield a far more natural result. During the
planning stage we saw that sampling audio-conditioned diffusion models was a
promising way to model this phenomenon in an ecologically valid manner ---
learning the distribution of a musician's imperfections rather than a fixed
delay. The obstacle was data: we could assemble only about two hours of paired
doubled takes, far too little to train a model that generalises. The MuScriptor
recipe above resolves exactly this: synthesise abundant doublings for
pre-training, then align the model with our small real set through reinforcement
learning. Unlike most generative tools now reaching the market, such a system
would \emph{assist} musicians rather than replace them, helping them widen a
performance they themselves played.

We began this thesis by arguing that, when scaling is out of reach, the fidelity
of the data becomes a productive vector for improvement. We have shown this in
three application fields --- hearing aids, speech enhancement and music source
separation --- and it may prove useful in others we did not set out to study. The
work presented here, however, has been largely incremental, and concerned with
offline, non-real-time algorithms; what we are ultimately drawn to is turning
these datasets and findings into deployable, interactive tools --- systems a
practitioner can use, rather than benchmarks alone.
