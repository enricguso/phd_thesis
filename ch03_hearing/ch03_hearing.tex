\chapter{An Objective Evaluation of Hearing Aids and DNN-based Binaural Speech Enhancement}
\chaptermark{Evaluation of Hearing Aids}
\label{ch03_hearing}
In broad terms, \acrshortpl{HA} are body-worn devices that capture the
acoustic scene with one or more microphones, processes it --- amplifying and compressing it according to the user's audiogram, and increasingly applying noise reduction and directional processing --- and deliver the result to the
ear through a small loudspeaker, conventionally called the \emph{receiver}. Devices are commonly grouped by form factor. In-The-Ear (ITE) \acrshortpl{HA} house the entire electronics in a custom shell that sits in the concha or ear canal; they are discreet but constrained in size, battery life and microphone spacing. \acrfull{BTE} \acrshortpl{HA} instead place the electronics in a unit worn behind the pinna, which routes the amplified sound to the ear through a tube ending in an earmould; the larger housing affords more processing power and a wider spacing between microphones, at the cost of visibility. The
\acrfull{RIC} style, now the most widespread, is a variant of the \acrshort{BTE} in which the receiver is removed from the housing and placed directly in the ear canal, connected to the behind-the-ear unit by a thin wire and sealed by a soft dome (Figure~\ref{fig:lewi_ric}). This separation keeps the \acrshort{BTE} unit small while moving the loudspeaker close to the eardrum, reducing the risk of acoustic feedback and improving sound quality --- which, together with its discreetness, explains its popularity and its choice for the present study.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\columnwidth,trim={6.8cm 11cm 5cm 11cm},clip]{ch03_hearing/figures/audifon.pdf}
  \caption{Schematic of the \emph{audifon lewi R}, a \acrfull{RIC} \acrshort{HA},
    the form factor used in this study. The behind-the-ear unit houses the
    microphones, battery and processor; a thin wire carries the signal to the

  \label{fig:lewi_ric}
\end{figure}

\acrshortpl{HA} are the primary clinical remedy for hearing
loss. Beyond restoring audibility through frequency-dependent amplification
and dynamic range compression, their practical value rests largely on a
harder problem: keeping speech intelligible amid competing sounds. To improve
the \acrshort{SNR}, \acrshortpl{HA} traditionally rely on directional microphones
\citep{ricketts1999} and adaptive beamformers
\citep{luo2002adaptive, gode2022}, complemented by single-channel
noise reduction. This enhancement is typically validated with stationary
noises under controlled laboratory conditions \citep{IEC6011816}. Everyday
listening, however --- a party, a restaurant, several competing talkers --- is
non-stationary and spatially complex, precisely the regime these methods
handle worst. The resulting mismatch between the conditions under which a
device is \emph{evaluated} and those in which it is \emph{used} is a question
of ecological validity, and closing it for \acrshortpl{HA} is the aim of
\textbf{RO1}.

In parallel, end-to-end \acrshortpl{DNN} (Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl}) have
transformed \acrshort{SE}, achieving strong denoising even in the
monaural case \citep{reddy2021icassp}. Their adoption in hearing devices has
been constrained by the requirement of causality and by tight compute and
latency budgets, but both are being addressed: causal architectures have been
proposed \citep{tzinis2020sudo} and scaled \citep{ali2023scaling}, binaural
models preserve spatial cues \citep{han2020}, algorithmic latencies now
approach those of conventional \acrshortpl{HA} processing
\citep{wang2023neural, stone2008delays}, and network miniaturisation
\citep{banbury2021micronets}, computation offloading \citep{sun2020supervised}
and the Clarity Challenge \citep{clarity} together make \acrshort{DNN}-based
\acrshortpl{HA} enhancement increasingly plausible. Indeed, some manufacturers
already embed \acrshort{DNN} post-filtering in commercial products
\citep{andersen2021, oticon2, oticon3}, yet without disclosing their
architectures or any ablation quantifying the gain they bring. Whether
\acrshortpl{DNN} genuinely generalise better than the mature, hand-engineered
enhancement of current devices, especially in the complex scenes where the
latter struggle, is therefore an open question and the object of
\textbf{RO2}.

This chapter, carried out as part of the GuestXR project in collaboration with Microson (Amplifon Group),
addresses both objectives with a single evaluation setup. \textbf{We
record five high-end commercial \acrshortpl{HA} exposed to complex
speech-in-noise scenes, reproduced over an Ambisonics loudspeaker array and captured on a \acrfull{KU100} dummy head,
and we compare their built-in enhancement against the same recordings
post-processed by two state-of-the-art \acrshortpl{DNN}}. 

The main contribution
is an evaluation which suggests that \acrshortpl{HA} traditional enhancement
struggles under challenging acoustic conditions, and that \acrshort{DNN}-based
\acrshort{SE} has the potential to improve current hearing devices.
Along the way, and helping to fill the absence of any public \acrshortpl{HA} evaluation dataset noted in Chapter~\ref{ch02_sota}, we release an
Ambisonics-to-binaural decoder tailored to \acrshortpl{HA}, a synthetic
binaural training corpus, and a reverberant speech-in-noise test set with the
corresponding recordings of each device.

\section{Hearing aids measurement setup}
\label{sec:ha_setup}

We first describe the measurement and evaluation infrastructure, deferring the
enhancement strategies it serves to compare to
Section~\ref{sec:ha_dnn}. The purpose here is only to explain how ecologically
valid scenes are generated, reproduced, captured on the \acrshortpl{HA}, and
turned into signals for the objective evaluation.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:ha_overview}

The setup, depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:ha_setup}, is designed to capture the
signals a \acrshort{HA} actually processes within complex acoustic scenes,
and comprises three stages. In \emph{scene generation}, realistic
speech-in-noise scenes are built by combining existing databases with
room-acoustic simulation, and rendered as sound fields in Ambisonics. In
\emph{recording}, these scenes are reproduced over an Ambisonics loudspeaker
layout and captured with the microphones of a dummy head wearing each \acrshort{HA}, with and without applying the device's enhancement features. In \emph{evaluation},
the \acrshortpl{HA}-enhanced recordings are compared against the same signals
processed by the \acrshortpl{DNN}, using a set of objective metrics computed
against a clean binaural reference. Next we detail each
component of the pipeline.

\subsection{Ambisonics complex scenes generation}
\label{sec:ha_scenes}

To ensure that the sound reaching each device closely resembles real life, all
scenes are represented and reproduced as Ambisonics sound fields
(Section~\ref{sec:bg_spatial}). We build three acoustic scenes of speech in
noise, corresponding to three common environments: \emph{party},
\emph{restaurant} and \emph{office}. The noise comes from the Ambisonic Recordings of Typical Environments (ARTE) database \citep{weisser2019ambisonic},
whose recordings were captured in real environments with a 62-channel
microphone array and distributed as 31-channel \emph{mixed-order} Ambisonics.

In a mixed-order representation, the horizontal plane is encoded at a higher
spherical-harmonic order than the vertical one, exploiting the fact that both
most sources and human localisation are concentrated near the horizontal:
full-sphere resolution is kept only up to a low order, and the
additional horizontal detail is carried by the sectorial harmonics
($m=\pm\ell$, the outermost components of the harmonic pyramid in
Figure~\ref{fig:spherical_harmonics}) alone. ARTE keeps the full
3D field up to 4th order (25 channels) and adds horizontal-only components up to 7th order (6 further channels), for 31 in total. We
zero-padded these into a full 10th-order set, placing each component in its
ACN slot and filling the remaining, unmeasured harmonics with
zeros. The target
speech consisted of nine sentences spoken by a female speaker, drawn from the
phonemically balanced Spanish Sharvard corpus \citep{aubanel2014shavrvard}.

Room acoustics are simulated with an adaptation of the \acrfull{MASP} library\footnote{\url{https://github.com/andresperezlopez/masp}}, a shoebox \acrshort{ISM} simulator (see Section~\ref{sec:bg_rir_ism})
extended to output the field directly in Ambisonics domain. \acrshort{MASP} is the translation from \citet*{politis2016microphone}'s MATLAB library into the Python programming language \citep{perez2020parametric}. Working at 10th order, which affords sufficient spatial
resolution, we simulate for each scene two sound fields at the left-ear,
right-ear and \emph{head} positions: an anechoic one, propagating only the
direct sound, and a reverberant one. The two ear positions were placed
$17.5\,\text{cm}$ apart to match the \acrshort{KU100} inter-ear distance. The
three rooms measured $15\times10\times3.5\,\text{m}$,
$28\times17\times4.2\,\text{m}$ and $5\times2\times2.5\,\text{m}$ for the
\emph{party}, \emph{restaurant} and \emph{office} scenes respectively, and the
target was placed $1\,\text{m}$ from the head at either $0^{\circ}$ or
$30^{\circ}$ to the right. The reverberation times were set by informal
listening against the ARTE recordings, choosing $T_{60}$ values
around $60\%$ of those reported for ARTE to account for the
absorption of furniture and people. The result is a set of clean (anechoic)
and reverberant speech and noise fields, all in Ambisonics.

\subsection{Decoding to the loudspeaker array}
\label{sec:ha_lsdecoding}

Playback took place in Eurecat's Spatial Audio recording and mixing studio, a three-dimensional
irregular array of 25 Genelec 8040 loudspeakers surrounding the listening
position. The studio is acoustically treated to keep its own reverberation short, but
it is not anechoic (Figure~\ref{fig:eurecat_studio}). The signals captured at the
dummy head therefore contain, on top of the reverberation deliberately
simulated into each scene, the small residual response of the playback room
itself; the acoustic treatment ensures this residual contribution stays well
below the intended, simulated acoustics that dominate the reproduced field. To reproduce a scene, the speech and noise sound fields are first
mixed at the \emph{head} position, weighting the two so that the binaural
signal-to-noise ratio is $+5\,\text{dB}$ once decoded to the ears (the
binaural decoder of Section~\ref{sec:ha_bindecoder}). The resulting Ambisonic
mixture is then decoded to the 25 loudspeaker feeds with a fifth-order
in-phase decoder (Section~\ref{sec:bg_ambi_timeline}) optimised with IDHOA
\citep{scaini2014decoding} for this particular irregular layout; the in-phase
design suppresses the side lobes of the virtual microphones, trading angular
sharpness for robustness away from the centre. Finally, all sets of
loudspeaker signals are normalised to equal energy to ease calibration.

\subsection{Dummy-head recordings}
\label{sec:ha_recordings}
A Neumann \acrshort{KU100} dummy head is placed at the centre of the array.
Each scene is calibrated to $70\,\text{dB}$ \acrfull{SPL} at the ear position,
next to the dummy head, using a Beyerdynamic MM1 omnidirectional measurement
microphone (Figure~\ref{fig:ha_dummyhead}). We record five high-end
\acrfull{RIC} \acrshortpl{HA} available on the market at the time of writing ---
the GN ONE 961-DRWC and 561-DRWC, the Phonak Audéo P90-R and P70-R, and the
Signia Pure C\&G 3x --- testing fifteen \acrshortpl{HA}–receiver combinations in
total, with low, mid and high-power receivers for each device. For each
recording, the \acrshortpl{HA} are fitted behind the ears of the \acrshort{KU100}
and their receivers are inserted into its ear canals; to minimise the
contribution of direct sound leaking past the device, the ear-canal entrance is
occluded with adhesive putty in addition to the \acrshortpl{HA}' own power dome.
Each scene is recorded twice with every \acrshort{HA}. In the first pass the
device is set to \emph{bypass}: all signal-enhancing algorithms are deactivated,
leaving only the feedback canceller and a linear amplification of approximately
$20\,\text{dB}$. Since commercial devices offer no direct access to their
microphone signals, these \emph{bypass} recordings at the \acrshort{KU100} serve
as an approximation of what the \acrshortpl{HA}' microphones capture, and are
therefore also the input fed to the offline \acrshort{DNN}-based enhancement of
Section~\ref{sec:ha_dnn}. In the second pass the device is set to \emph{enabled},
applying the signal-enhancement algorithms of its default factory settings,
which in all tested devices include at least some form of adaptive beamforming
and single-channel noise reduction. No hearing correction is applied in either
mode.

\subsection{Ambisonics-to-binaural hearing aids decoder}
\label{sec:ha_bindecoder}

Returning to the evaluation pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:ha_setup}, the one
element missing before being able to evaluate the performance is a way to obtain the clean references against which the
metrics are computed. The intrusive metrics below require a clean binaural
reference matching what the \acrshortpl{HA}' microphones would capture in the
absence of noise and reverberation. Since our scenes live in the Ambisonic
domain --- including the anechoic speech field simulated in MASP
(Section~\ref{sec:ha_scenes}) --- obtaining that reference amounts to decoding
Ambisonics to binaural, which in turn calls for a set of \acrshort{HRTF}s
specific to the device-on-head configuration. Although \citet{oldenburg}
published \acrshort{HRTF}s for the microphones of a \acrshort{BTE} \acrshortpl{HA} on a head-and-torso simulator, none were available for the \acrshort{KU100}, and, evaluating commercial devices, we had no access to their microphone signals. We therefore measured our own \acrshort{HRTF}s in a configuration as close as possible to the recording setup: in the same room, with a pair of \emph{audifon lewi R} \acrshortpl{HA} coupled to the dummy-head ear canals, enhancement features disabled and only a linear gain applied.

The \acrshort{HRTF}s were measured with the exponential-sweep method
(Section~\ref{sec:bg_rir_sweep}) using a single Genelec 8020 loudspeaker
positioned at each of the 50 directions of a \emph{Lebedev grid}
(Figure~\ref{fig:lebedev}). Lebedev grids are sets of directions distributed as evenly as possible over
the sphere, arranged so that each one has its mirror image across every axis.
This symmetry covers all directions uniformly with comparatively few points:
the 50 directions comprise the $6$ vertices of an octahedron, the $8$ vertices
of a cube, the $12$ edge midpoints between them, and a further symmetric
orbit of $24$ points. Grouped by elevation, they form horizontal rings whose
population grows towards the horizontal plane --- a single point at each pole,
four at intermediate elevations and eight on the rings nearest and on the
horizontal --- since the rings near the equator are longer circles and fit
more points at an even spacing. This concentrates angular resolution around
the horizontal plane, where human localisation is most acute. Such efficiency
is convenient for \acrshort{HRTF} acquisition, where every added direction
means another physical measurement.



The measured impulse responses were windowed before the arrival of the first
wall reflection, yielding quasi-anechoic responses, and their low-frequency
content was extended with the spherical-model \acrfull{LFE} of
\citet{bernschutz2013spherical}. From these we build a 10th-order
Ambisonics-to-binaural decoder following the \acrshort{BiMagLS} method~\citep{engel2021improving} (Section~\ref{sec:bg_ambi_timeline}), with
spherical-harmonic tapering above a cutoff of $6239\,\text{Hz}$ --- the
theoretical limit for correct representation at 10th order. The clean binaural
reference is then obtained by decoding the anechoic
left and right-ear sound fields simulated in \acrshort{MASP}. The measured \acrshort{HRTF}s are released in the \acrfull{SOFA} format
\citep{aes69}. \acrshort{SOFA}\footnote{we use the Python library from \url{https://github.com/Jencke/pySOFA}}, standardised as AES69, is a self-describing container that stores acoustic impulse responses --- here the
\acrshort{HRIR} pairs --- together with the full measurement geometry (source
and receiver positions and orientations) and metadata, so that a dataset can
be exchanged and reused across tools without ambiguity.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:ha_metrics}
The objective evaluation compares four sets of recordings: \emph{bypass},
\emph{enabled}, \emph{bypass} post-processed with the non-causal model
(\textbf{\acrshort{DNN}}) and \emph{bypass} post-processed with its causal
counterpart (\textbf{\acrshort{DNN}-C}). The first represents the absence of
signal enhancement and the remaining three the different enhancement strategies
under comparison.

In a preliminary round we attempted to estimate the output \acrshort{SNR} with
the phase-inversion procedure of \citet{hagerman2004method}. The idea is to
present the same scene twice: first the mixture of speech and noise, and then
the same mixture with the \emph{noise inverted in polarity}. If the device
applies the same processing in both passes, adding the two recordings cancels
the noise and leaves twice the processed speech, while subtracting them cancels
the speech and leaves twice the processed noise. Having isolated the two
components at the output, the output \acrshort{SNR} --- and hence the improvement
over the input --- follows directly. The procedure, however, assumes that the
system behaves identically across the two presentations; this did not hold for
several \acrshortpl{HA}, whose adaptive processing reacted differently to the
polarity-inverted noise, nor for any of the \acrshortpl{DNN}, whose non-linear
mapping breaks the assumption outright, making the estimates unreliable. We
therefore rely on intrusive, reference-based metrics computed against the clean
binaural reference $y$ of Section~\ref{sec:ha_bindecoder}.

Given an enhanced estimate $\tilde{y}$ and the corresponding unprocessed
recording $\hat{y}$, we time-align each pair to the reference by
cross-correlation and compute four objective measures: the \acrfull{HASQI}
\citep{hasqi}, the \acrfull{HASPI} \citep{haspi}, the \acrfull{MBSTOI}
\citep{andersen2018refinement} and the \acrfull{SI-SDR} \citep{le2019sdr}. For
the non-binaural measures (\acrshort{SI-SDR}, \acrshort{HASPI}, \acrshort{HASQI})
we take the better ear and normalise the signals as in \citet{clarity}, using a
flat normal-hearing audiogram as input to \acrshort{HASPI} and \acrshort{HASQI}.
Because \acrshort{HASPI} and \acrshort{HASQI} rest on a level-dependent auditory
model, they require a calibration relating digital amplitude to presentation
level; both reference and estimate are scaled so that their root mean square
match the $70\,\text{dB}$ \acrshort{SPL} at which the scenes are reproduced, rather than the $65\,\text{dB}$ \acrshort{SPL}
assumed by default for a signal with Root Mean Square of one. The
scale-invariant \acrshort{SI-SDR} is defined as,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR}(\tilde{y}, y) =
    10\log_{10}\frac{\lVert \alpha\, y \rVert^{2}}
                    {\lVert \alpha\, y - \tilde{y} \rVert^{2}},
    \qquad
    \alpha = \frac{\tilde{y}^{\top} y}{\lVert y \rVert^{2}},
    \label{eq:sisdr}
\end{equation}
where the optimal scaling $\alpha$ makes the measure invariant to the overall
gain of the estimate.

The remaining three measures are intrusive quality and intelligibility
predictors specific to this domain. \acrshort{HASQI} and \acrshort{HASPI} were
designed to predict, respectively, perceived \emph{quality} and
\emph{intelligibility} for both normal-hearing and hearing-impaired listeners:
each passes the reference and the estimate through an auditory model that
incorporates the listener's audiogram --- here a flat normal-hearing one --- and
compares the two along cepstral and temporal-envelope features, returning a score
in $[0, 1]$ where $1$ denotes a signal indistinguishable from the reference.
\acrshort{MBSTOI} \citep{andersen2018refinement} is the binaural extension of the
short-time objective intelligibility measure: it correlates the short-time
temporal envelopes of reference and estimate within frequency bands and across
the two ears --- thereby rewarding the preservation of interaural cues --- and
likewise yields a score in $[0, 1]$, monotonically related to predicted
intelligibility. Unlike \acrshort{SI-SDR}, which is a generic signal-fidelity
ratio in decibels, these three are perceptually motivated and bounded, which is
why we report them alongside it.

Because absolute metric values conflate the enhancement with the difficulty of
the scene, we report the \emph{signal-enhancement benefit}: the improvement an
enhancement brings over the unprocessed recording. For \acrshort{SI-SDR}, for
instance,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SI\text{-}SDRi} = \mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR}(\tilde{y}, y) - \mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR}(\hat{y}, y),
    \label{eq:benefit}
\end{equation}
and $\Delta\mathrm{HASQI}$, $\Delta\mathrm{HASPI}$ and $\Delta\mathrm{MBSTOI}$ are
defined analogously. A positive benefit means the enhancement improves on doing
nothing; a negative one means it degrades the signal.

\section{DNN-based binaural speech enhancement models}
\label{sec:ha_dnn}

The recording procedure described so far already enables to evaluate 
the \acrshortpl{HA}' own \acrshort{SE}. Our aim, however, is to compare it against a prototype \acrshort{DNN}-based post-filter, so we now turn to training a binaural \acrshort{SE} model. We approach it as a
\emph{supervised} learning problem (Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_advent}), which
requires a large collection of noisy, reverberant mixtures paired with their
corresponding clean-speech targets. Both inputs and targets are \emph{binaural}, so that the model can exploit the
spatial cues a hearing-aid user relies on (Section~\ref{sec:bg_spatial_paradigms})
instead of destroying them. Additionally, both must resemble the signals that the
two frontal microphones of a hearing-aid device would capture, which is
precisely what the \acrshortpl{HA} \acrshort{BiMagLS} decoder of
Section~\ref{sec:ha_bindecoder} provides.

\subsection{Speech and noise material}
\label{sec:ha_data}

Following common practice in the \acrshort{SE} literature
\citep{reddy2021icassp, tzinis2020sudo}, the clean speech comes from audiobook
recordings: specifically the Spanish subset of \acrfull{MLS} \citep{pratap2020mls},
chosen to match the language of the Sharvard sentences used in the evaluation
scenes. All material is resampled to $16\,\text{kHz}$ and split into four-second
chunks; for each utterance we retain the chunk with the highest energy, which
avoids training on silence. This yields $221$k utterances for training, $2408$
for validation and $2385$ for testing, amounting to some $251$ hours of clean
speech as shown in Table~\ref{tab:ha_dataset}.

The noise comes from WHAM! \citep{wichern2019wham}, a binaural corpus of real
ambient recordings --- babble, cafeteria noise and background music --- captured
in urban environments, and thus a good match for the everyday situations the
\acrshortpl{HA} are evaluated in. We keep the original splits of both corpora,
which preserve gender balance and prevent speaker contamination across sets. Because WHAM! is considerably smaller than \acrshort{MLS}, its training split is
augmented to match the total duration of the speech material through three
strategies: \emph{(i)} inverting the polarity of the noise signal, \emph{(ii)}
swapping its left and right channels, a rough approximation of a $180^{\circ}$
rotation of the noise field in the horizontal plane, and \emph{(iii)}
time-stretching it by a random factor between $90\%$ and $110\%$. Combining them
yields the six subsets of Table~\ref{tab:ha_dataset}, whose $245.6$ hours match
the speech training set exactly. Validation and test noise is drawn, unaugmented,
from the corresponding WHAM! splits.

\subsection{Binaural room simulation}
\label{sec:ha_simulation}

\acrshort{MLS} consists of close-microphone studio recordings, which we take
as a fair approximation of anechoic signals and therefore as suitable dry
sources for simulation. Each training example is then built by placing a
speech source and the listener inside a randomly drawn shoebox room, simulating
the corresponding sound fields with \acrshort{MASP}
(Section~\ref{sec:ha_scenes}) and decoding them to binaural with the
\acrshortpl{HA} \acrshort{BiMagLS} decoder of Section~\ref{sec:ha_bindecoder} ---
the very same pipeline used to generate the evaluation scenes, keeping
training and test data consistent.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

The random configurations are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:ha_rooms}. Two of its
choices deserve comment, as both encode acoustic common sense rather than
independent sampling. The room depth $r_y$ is drawn \emph{relative} to its
width $r_x$, which prevents the sampler from generating implausible,
corridor-like geometries. More subtly, the reverberation time is made to depend
on the \acrshort{SNR}: noisier scenes are assigned shorter $T_{60}$ values, on
the grounds that a room crowded enough to be noisy is also a room whose
occupants absorb a substantial part of its reverberation. Sampling the two
independently would produce many physically implausible combinations --- a
loud, crowded room with a long, empty-hall decay --- and training on them would
waste capacity on situations the model will never meet.

One further correction is applied. Decoding through our own measured
\acrshort{HRTF}s imprints on every training example the particular colouration
of the \emph{audifon lewi R} device coupled to the \acrshort{KU100} ear canal
--- an artefact of our measurement rather than a property of \acrshortpl{HA}. We compensated the response of the \acrshort{RIC} coupling by taking the direction-independent frequency response between the
\acrshort{HRTF}s of the bare \acrshort{KU100} and those of the \acrshort{KU100}
wearing the \acrshortpl{HA}, approximating it with an Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) filter, and
applying that filter to every utterance in the dataset.

\subsection{Training setup}
\label{sec:ha_training}

As the enhancement network we adopted \emph{SuDo-RM-RF} \citep{tzinis2020sudo},
a well-tested architecture originally designed for source separation
that has also performed well on \acrshortpl{HA} \acrshort{SE} \citep{leecitear, clarity}. 
It follows the mask-based, time-domain paradigm of
Conv-TasNet \citep{luo2019conv} from  Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_temporal}: a learned one-dimensional
convolutional encoder maps the waveform into a latent representation, a
separator estimates a mask over it, and a decoder reconstructs the enhanced
waveform, thus avoiding the magnitude--phase compromise of spectrogram-domain
models. Its main building block is the U-ConvBlock, which --- like the encoder-decoder structure
of the U-Net of Section~\ref{sec:bg_dl_advent} --- successively downsamples its
input to extract features at multiple temporal resolutions and then resamples
and sums them back, so that a wide temporal context is aggregated with far
fewer parameters and operations than a deep stack of dilated convolutions would
require. This efficiency is what makes the architecture attractive for a
\acrshortpl{HA} setting.


We train two variants. \emph{DNN} is the non-causal configuration named \emph{SuDoRM-RF++GC} on their paper, which may look into future samples and
therefore serves as an upper baseline on what the architecture can achieve.
\emph{\acrshort{DNN}-C} is its causal counterpart, \emph{C-SuDoRM-RF++}, in which
convolutions and normalisation only access present and past samples; it is the variant that could conceivably
run on a \acrshortpl{HA} device. Beyond configuring the encoder to accept two-channel
input, the topology was left unmodified.

Both models use 256 input and 512 output channels across 16 successive
U-ConvBlocks with five downsampling/upsampling layers each, encoder and decoder
kernels of size 21 producing embeddings of length 512, and four attention heads
of depth 256 with a dropout of $0.1$ applied during training only. All
hyperparameters are shared between the two models except for the batch size, which
had to be reduced from 12 for \emph{\acrshort{DNN}-C} to 2 for \emph{DNN} to fit in GPU
memory. We optimize with Adam at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, divided by
three every eight epochs, for 25 epochs over the whole dataset. Fresh mixtures
are generated at every epoch by permuting the reverberant speech and the noise
within each batch, which effectively works as an additional augmentation, as no pairing is
ever seen twice,  and each mixture is normalised to zero mean and unit
variance. We use the negative \acrshort{SI-SDR} as the training loss and \acrshort{SI-SDR} itself as the validation metric, reaching $11.7\,\text{dB}$ on the test split.

\label{sec:ha_results}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:ha_discussion}

In this chapter we have presented a setup which allows to quantify the performance gap between the current \acrshortpl{HA} signal enhancement strategies and DNN-based approaches. On the one hand, HA processing seems to struggle in these complex situations, even performing worse than in \textit{bypass} for some devices, perhaps because beamformers cannot estimate the direction of the target speech due to the noise and competing talkers being non-stationary. On the other hand, this does not seem to affect \acrshortpl{DNN}, which improve SISDR and MBSTOI consistently. Interestingly, differences between devices in \textit{bypass} are still observed after being processed by the \acrshortpl{DNN}, suggesting that DNN models are sensitive to the quality of the inputs. It would be interesting to study the concatenation of \acrshortpl{DNN} with traditional \acrshortpl{HA} strategies in future work.

We acknowledge that using the binaural decoding of the anechoic signal as reference can seem counter-intuitive because \acrshortpl{HA}
are not designed to provide anechoic estimates yet. However, this
reference was the one that matched better with our informal listening. Another limitation of the study is that we are comparing
real time \acrshortpl{HA} processing with \acrshortpl{DNN} applied as a post-processing,
because we found it difficult to obtain signal insert points in consumer HA devices and also because no real time implementations
of these models are publicly available yet. However, the slight performance difference between \emph{DNN} and \emph{DNN-C} and both being far
better than the HA in terms of \acrshort{SI-SDR} and \acrshort{MBSTOI} should additionally encourage this research direction.


\section{Follow-up work}
\label{sec:ha_followup}

The evaluation presented here has since been complemented by a perceptual study,
published at Forum Acusticum Euronoise~2025 \citep{baig2025perceptual} and carried
out by Mart\'i Baig as part of his Master's thesis. Being his contribution, it
lies beyond the scope of this dissertation, and we summarise it here only for
completeness. Using the same recordings and models, it runs a \acrfull{MUSHRA}-like
listening test with two groups of twenty participants --- \acrshortpl{HA} users
with moderate-to-severe hearing loss and normal-hearing listeners --- rating the
six processing conditions (\emph{bypass}, \emph{enabled}, and the causal and
non-causal \acrshortpl{DNN}) across the six scenes, with the \emph{bypass}
recording as the hidden reference. Crucially, it adds a \emph{mild} variant of
both networks, trained on \emph{pseudo-anechoic} targets that remove the noise
while preserving some reverberation, in contrast to the \emph{strong} variants
studied in this chapter, whose fully anechoic targets suppress it entirely.

Both groups prefer the mild \acrshort{DNN} models over every other condition ---
the non-causal mild model ranking first, and the causal one, the only variant
that could run on a device, close behind --- while the strong variants of this
chapter are rated lowest of all, criticised for overly aggressive suppression
that sacrifices naturalness and spatial perception. The \acrshortpl{HA}' own
\emph{enabled} processing scores only moderately and with high variability.
Statistically, the robust effect is precisely this contrast between mild and
strong enhancement, rather than a clean separation between conventional and
\acrshort{DNN} processing; and a re-computation of the objective metrics with
each listener's own audiogram broadly tracks these preferences. Two conclusions
carry back to the present work. First, the subjective results support the
objective finding that \acrshort{DNN} enhancement has the potential to improve on
current \acrshortpl{HA} processing in complex scenes. Second, they indicate that
the anechoic reference adopted here, flagged among the limitations in
Section~\ref{sec:ha_discussion}, is indeed more aggressive than \acrshortpl{HA}
users would choose: a pseudo-anechoic target that leaves some reverberation
intact appears to be the more ecologically sensible design, and a promising
direction for the speech-enhancement work of the following chapter.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:ha_conclusions}

This chapter has approached \acrshortpl{HA} \acrshort{SE} as a problem of
\emph{ecological validity}: conventional devices are certified under stationary,
laboratory conditions, yet users struggle precisely where everyday listening is
non-stationary and spatially complex. By reproducing such scenes over an
Ambisonic loudspeaker array and capturing them on real devices, we evaluated under conditions closer to real use, and
could compare five \acrshortpl{HA} devices against \acrshort{DNN}-based \acrshort{SE}.

Regarding \textbf{RO1}, this ecologically valid evaluation exposed a clear
performance gap: the signal-enhancement algorithms of the five devices struggle
in complex, real-world speech-in-noise scenes, in several cases degrading the
signal relative to applying no enhancement at all. 

Regarding \textbf{RO2}, we have shown that \acrshort{DNN}-based \acrfull{SE} has the potential to outperform
them substantially in denoising and objective intelligibility, and that the
causal variant retains
most of that advantage. Together, these findings encourage further work on the
optimisation and pruning of such models for on-device deployment, and on their
subjective validation with hearing-impaired listeners, like the follow-up study
of Section~\ref{sec:ha_followup}.

Beyond the evaluation itself, the chapter contributes the infrastructure that
made it possible, released as research
materials\footnote{\url{https://github.com/enricguso/guso_waspaa23}}: a set of
\acrshortpl{HRTF} measured on a \acrshort{KU100} wearing a \acrshort{RIC} \acrshort{HA}, 
the corresponding Ambisonics-to-binaural \acrshort{BiMagLS} decoder, a
synthetic binaural training corpus for \acrshort{HA} \acrshort{SE}, and a
reverberant speech-in-noise test set in Ambisonics and binaural formats, together
with the recordings of each device.
